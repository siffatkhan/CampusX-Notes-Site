7. Vanishing & Exploding Gradients

Why gradients shrink to nothing (or blow up) in deep networks, how to spot it, and how to fix it

Day 14

The Vanishing Gradient Problem (VGP)

The chain rule in backpropagation, as the previous chapter's worked example showed, computes a weight's gradient by multiplying together a chain of small derivative terms — one for every layer the error signal has to pass back through. That multiplication is exactly where trouble starts.

The core intuition: multiplying several small numbers together produces an even smaller number. \( 0.1 \times 0.1 \times 0.1 = 0.001 \) — three layers already shrinks the signal a thousandfold. Stack ten or twenty layers, each contributing another small multiplicative factor, and the gradient reaching the earliest layers becomes vanishingly close to zero.

This is why the problem shows up almost exclusively in deep networks with many hidden layers — a shallow network simply doesn't have enough multiplicative hops for the effect to matter. It is also strongly tied to which activation function is used. Sigmoid squeezes every output into the range [0, 1], and its derivative is at most 0.25 (reached only at \( z=0 \), and rapidly approaching 0 for large positive or negative \( z \)). Tanh's derivative peaks at 1 but likewise collapses toward 0 away from the origin. Chain several layers of either together, and every layer multiplies in another factor that is at best a quarter (sigmoid) or a fraction close to zero once activations saturate — so the gradient reaching layer 1 in a 10-layer sigmoid network can be astronomically smaller than the gradient at layer 10.

The practical consequence: when a weight's gradient is effectively zero, its gradient descent update \( w_{new} = w_{old} - \alpha \cdot \partial L/\partial w \) barely changes it at all. Early layers of the network — often the ones responsible for learning the most basic, foundational features — simply stop learning, even while later layers may still be updating normally.

Day 14

Recognizing the Vanishing Gradient Problem

Watch the loss

If the training loss stops changing — plateaus early, and stays flat for many epochs despite continued training — that flatness is one of the clearest symptoms that gradients have vanished and weights have effectively stopped updating.

Plot the weights

Plotting the distribution or magnitude of weights in the earlier layers, across training steps, is the more direct diagnostic — if those weights barely move from their initial values while later-layer weights are updating normally, the gradient isn't reaching them.

Day 14

Five Ways to Handle the Vanishing Gradient Problem

1. Reduce model depth

Using a shallow network instead of a deep one with many layers removes multiplicative hops, so gradients survive the trip back — but this is a poor trade in practice. The entire reason to reach for a deep network in the first place is to capture complex, hierarchical patterns that a shallow network structurally cannot represent. Shrinking depth to dodge VGP just trades one failure (vanishing gradients) for another (insufficient model capacity) — it "solves" the symptom by giving up the reason the network was deep to begin with.

2. Use the ReLU activation function

ReLU is defined as \( \text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z) \): zero for any negative input, and the input itself for any positive input. Its derivative is either exactly 0 (for negative \(z\)) or exactly 1 (for positive \(z\)) — never a fraction like sigmoid's ≤0.25. Multiplying by 1 repeatedly across layers doesn't shrink anything, so gradients pass through the positive region of a ReLU network essentially undiminished, which is the main reason ReLU became the default hidden-layer activation for deep networks.

The trade-off: that same "0 for negative input" behavior creates the dying ReLU problem — if a neuron's weighted input keeps landing in negative territory, its output and gradient are permanently zero, and gradient descent can never nudge it back to life. Chapter 9 covers this in depth, along with the ReLU variants designed to fix it.

3. Proper weight initialization

Techniques like Xavier/Glorot and He initialization choose the starting scale of each layer's weights based on the number of inputs and outputs of that layer, so that activations and gradients start out with roughly consistent variance as they pass through the network, instead of shrinking or exploding purely as an artifact of poor starting values. Chapter 10 works through the exact formulas.

4. Batch normalization

A batch normalization layer re-centers and re-scales its inputs to have roughly zero mean and unit variance at every layer, mini-batch by mini-batch. Keeping activations in a well-behaved range throughout the network prevents them from drifting into the saturated, near-flat regions of sigmoid/tanh where derivatives collapse toward zero. Chapter 10 covers the mechanics in full.

5. Residual (skip) connections

A residual connection lets a layer's input skip ahead and be added directly to a later layer's output, giving the gradient an alternate path back to earlier layers that doesn't have to pass through every intermediate multiplication. This building block is used in ordinary deep ANNs, but its most famous application is in CNNs — ResNet (Residual Network) is built almost entirely out of stacked residual blocks, which is precisely what let image-classification networks scale to over a hundred layers without the earlier layers' gradients vanishing.

Day 14

The Exploding Gradient Problem

Exploding gradients are the mirror image of vanishing gradients, and come from the exact same chain-rule multiplication mechanism, just in the opposite direction: instead of repeatedly multiplying numbers smaller than 1 (which shrinks toward zero), the chain repeatedly multiplies numbers larger than 1 — and a product of many large numbers grows enormous very quickly. A gradient of, say, 2 at each of 20 layers compounds to \( 2^{20} \approx \) one million times its original size by the time it reaches the earliest layer.

This is most commonly seen in RNNs (Recurrent Neural Networks), where the same weight matrix is applied repeatedly across many time steps — effectively creating an extremely deep chain of multiplications by the same values, which readily runs away in either direction depending on whether those values are, on average, less than or greater than 1. When gradients explode, weight updates become wildly large, and training visibly destabilizes — loss spikes erratically or turns into NaN.

Extended note — the source notes describe the exploding gradient problem but don't name a fix for it. The standard remedy, used almost universally in RNN training, is added here: gradient clipping — rescaling the gradient vector whenever its norm exceeds some threshold, so it points in the same direction but its magnitude is capped. This bounds how large a single weight update can ever be, without changing the direction gradient descent is trying to move in. 
import tensorflow as tf

# gradient clipping by global norm — the standard fix for exploding gradients
optimizer = tf.keras.optimizers.Adam(learning_rate=0.001, clipnorm=1.0)

model.compile(optimizer=optimizer, loss='mse')
model.fit(X_train, y_train, epochs=20)

Hard

5 questions on Vanishing & Exploding Gradients. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. Why does the vanishing gradient problem mainly affect deep networks rather than shallow ones?

Explanation: The gradient at an early layer is a product of the derivative terms from every layer between it and the output. Few layers means few multiplications and little shrinkage; many layers compounds it — just like 0.1×0.1×0.1 shrinking much faster than 0.1×0.1.

2. Sigmoid's derivative has a maximum value of about 0.25. What does this imply for a deep sigmoid network?

Explanation: A derivative that never exceeds 0.25 means every layer of a sigmoid-based deep network can only shrink the backward gradient — never preserve or amplify it — making vanishing gradients close to inevitable as depth grows.

3. Why is "just use a shallow network" considered a poor fix for the vanishing gradient problem?

Explanation: Deep networks are used specifically to capture complex, hierarchical patterns a shallow network can't represent. Reducing depth avoids vanishing gradients but reintroduces the exact capacity limitation deep learning was meant to overcome.

4. What is the "dying ReLU" trade-off that comes with using ReLU to fight vanishing gradients?

Explanation: ReLU's derivative is 0 for any negative input. If a neuron always receives a negative weighted sum, its gradient is always 0, so gradient descent can never move its weights again — the neuron is permanently "dead."

5. Why are RNNs especially prone to exploding (and vanishing) gradients?

Explanation: Unrolled over time, an RNN reuses the same weights at every step, so backpropagation through time chains together many multiplications of essentially the same values — depending on their scale, this compounds toward zero (vanishing) or infinity (exploding) very quickly.
