7. Vanishing & Exploding Gradients

Why gradients shrink to nothing (or blow up) in deep networks, how to spot it, and how to fix it

Day 14

The Vanishing Gradient Problem (VGP)

The chain rule in backpropagation, as the previous chapter's worked example showed, computes a weight's gradient by multiplying together a chain of small derivative terms. You multiply one derivative for every layer the error signal passes back through. This is where the trouble starts.

The core intuition: multiply several small numbers together and you get an even smaller number. \( 0.1 \times 0.1 \times 0.1 = 0.001 \). Three layers already shrink the signal a thousandfold. Stack ten or twenty layers, each contributing another small multiplicative factor, and the gradient reaching the earliest layers gets close to zero.

This is why the problem shows up almost exclusively in deep networks with many hidden layers. A shallow network doesn't have enough multiplicative hops for the effect to matter. The activation function you use matters too. Sigmoid squeezes every output into the range [0, 1], and its derivative is at most 0.25 (reached only at \( z=0 \), and dropping fast toward 0 for large positive or negative \( z \)). Tanh's derivative peaks at 1 but also collapses toward 0 away from the origin. Chain several layers of either together, and every layer multiplies in another factor that is at best a quarter (sigmoid) or close to zero once activations saturate. The gradient reaching layer 1 in a 10-layer sigmoid network ends up far smaller than the gradient at layer 10.

The practical consequence: when a weight's gradient is effectively zero, the gradient descent update \( w_{new} = w_{old} - \alpha \cdot \partial L/\partial w \) barely changes it. Early layers of the network, often the ones that learn the most basic, foundational features, stop learning. Later layers, meanwhile, keep updating normally.

Day 14

Recognizing the Vanishing Gradient Problem

Watch the loss

If the training loss stops changing, plateaus early, and stays flat for many epochs despite continued training, that flatness is one of the clearest symptoms that gradients have vanished and weights have effectively stopped updating.

Plot the weights

Plotting the distribution or magnitude of weights in the earlier layers, across training steps, gives a more direct diagnostic. If those weights barely move from their initial values while later-layer weights update normally, the gradient isn't reaching them.

Day 14

Five Ways to Handle the Vanishing Gradient Problem

1. Reduce model depth

A shallow network removes multiplicative hops, so gradients survive the trip back. But this is a poor trade in practice. You reach for a deep network to capture complex, hierarchical patterns that a shallow network is structurally unable to represent. Shrinking depth to dodge VGP trades one failure (vanishing gradients) for another (insufficient model capacity). It solves the symptom by giving up the reason the network was deep in the first place.

2. Use the ReLU activation function

ReLU is defined as \( \text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z) \): zero for any negative input, and the input itself for any positive input. Its derivative is either exactly 0 (for negative \(z\)) or exactly 1 (for positive \(z\)), never a fraction like sigmoid's ≤0.25. Multiplying by 1 repeatedly across layers doesn't shrink anything, so gradients pass through the positive region of a ReLU network essentially undiminished. This is the main reason ReLU became the default hidden-layer activation for deep networks.

The trade-off: that same zero-for-negative-input behavior creates the dying ReLU problem. If a neuron's weighted input keeps landing in negative territory, its output and gradient stay permanently zero, and gradient descent never nudges it back to life. Chapter 9 covers this in depth, along with the ReLU variants designed to fix it.

3. Proper weight initialization

Techniques like Xavier/Glorot and He initialization choose the starting scale of each layer's weights based on the number of inputs and outputs of that layer. This keeps activations and gradients at roughly consistent variance as they pass through the network, instead of shrinking or exploding purely because of poor starting values. Chapter 10 works through the exact formulas.

4. Batch normalization

A batch normalization layer re-centers and re-scales its inputs to have roughly zero mean and unit variance at every layer, mini-batch by mini-batch. Keeping activations in a well-behaved range throughout the network stops them from drifting into the saturated, near-flat regions of sigmoid/tanh where derivatives collapse toward zero. Chapter 10 covers the mechanics in full.

5. Residual (skip) connections

A residual connection lets a layer's input skip ahead and add directly to a later layer's output. This gives the gradient an alternate path back to earlier layers that doesn't have to pass through every intermediate multiplication. Ordinary deep ANNs use this building block, but its most famous application is in CNNs. ResNet (Residual Network) is built almost entirely out of stacked residual blocks. This is what let image-classification networks scale to over a hundred layers without the earlier layers' gradients vanishing.

Day 14

The Exploding Gradient Problem

Exploding gradients are the mirror image of vanishing gradients. They come from the exact same chain-rule multiplication mechanism, just in the opposite direction. Instead of repeatedly multiplying numbers smaller than 1 (which shrinks toward zero), the chain repeatedly multiplies numbers larger than 1, and a product of many large numbers grows enormous fast. A gradient of, say, 2 at each of 20 layers compounds to \( 2^{20} \approx \) one million times its original size by the time it reaches the earliest layer.

This is most commonly seen in RNNs (Recurrent Neural Networks), where the same weight matrix applies repeatedly across many time steps. This effectively creates a long chain of multiplications by the same values, which runs away in either direction depending on whether those values are, on average, less than or greater than 1. When gradients explode, weight updates become wildly large, and training visibly destabilizes. Loss spikes erratically or turns into NaN.

Extended note: the source notes describe the exploding gradient problem but don't name a fix for it. The standard remedy, used almost universally in RNN training, is added here: gradient clipping. This rescales the gradient vector whenever its norm exceeds some threshold, so it points in the same direction but its magnitude is capped. This bounds how large a single weight update ever gets, without changing the direction gradient descent moves in. 
import tensorflow as tf

# gradient clipping by global norm — the standard fix for exploding gradients
optimizer = tf.keras.optimizers.Adam(learning_rate=0.001, clipnorm=1.0)

model.compile(optimizer=optimizer, loss='mse')
model.fit(X_train, y_train, epochs=20)

Test Your Understanding

Hard

5 questions on Vanishing & Exploding Gradients. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. Why does the vanishing gradient problem mainly affect deep networks rather than shallow ones?

Explanation: The gradient at an early layer is a product of the derivative terms from every layer between it and the output. Few layers means few multiplications and little shrinkage. Many layers compounds it, just like 0.1×0.1×0.1 shrinks much faster than 0.1×0.1.

2. Sigmoid's derivative has a maximum value of about 0.25. What does this imply for a deep sigmoid network?

Explanation: A derivative that never exceeds 0.25 means every layer of a sigmoid-based deep network only shrinks the backward gradient. It never preserves or amplifies it. This makes vanishing gradients close to inevitable as depth grows.

3. Why is "just use a shallow network" considered a poor fix for the vanishing gradient problem?

Explanation: Deep networks capture complex, hierarchical patterns a shallow network is unable to represent. Reducing depth avoids vanishing gradients but reintroduces the exact capacity limitation deep learning was meant to overcome.

4. What is the "dying ReLU" trade-off that comes with using ReLU to fight vanishing gradients?

Explanation: ReLU's derivative is 0 for any negative input. If a neuron always receives a negative weighted sum, its gradient stays 0, so gradient descent never moves its weights again. The neuron stays permanently dead.

5. Why are RNNs especially prone to exploding (and vanishing) gradients?

Explanation: Unrolled over time, an RNN reuses the same weights at every step, so backpropagation through time chains together many multiplications of essentially the same values. Depending on their scale, this compounds toward zero (vanishing) or infinity (exploding) fast.
