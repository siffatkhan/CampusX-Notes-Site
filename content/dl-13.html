LeNet to ResNet · the classic CNN pipeline · feature extraction vs. fine-tuning

Architecture

The Classic CNN Pipeline

Almost every classic CNN built for image classification follows the same overall shape. It doesn't matter how deep or elaborate the specific architecture gets. Raw pixels go in one end, a class prediction comes out the other, with a fixed sequence of transformations in between.

IMAGE
↓
Convolutional layers (+ padding/strides + pooling, repeated)
↓
Flatten
↓
Fully Connected (Dense) layers
↓
OUTPUT (class probabilities)

The convolutional stage does all the feature-learning work. It alternates convolution, padding/stride choices, and pooling, as covered on the previous two pages, and progressively turns raw pixels into a compact set of high-level features. Flatten then collapses that final 3D feature volume into a 1D vector. The dense layers at the end do the same job a plain ANN would: they combine those features into a decision. Every architecture below is a different, increasingly sophisticated way to design the convolutional stage of this same pipeline.

Architecture

A Tour of the Classic Architectures

LeNet

Yann LeCun designed LeNet, often called the father of CNNs. It was the original architecture that showed convolutional networks could reliably recognize handwritten digits. Structurally, it's the classic pipeline above in its simplest form: a small number of alternating convolutional and pooling layers extract features from the digit image, followed by a couple of dense layers that map those features to one of the 10 digit classes. It's tiny by modern standards, but it proved the core idea every architecture below builds on: learned filters beat hand-engineered features.

AlexNet

AlexNet, a much deeper and wider descendant of LeNet, made deep CNNs mainstream. It won the ImageNet competition by a huge margin. This showed that a sufficiently deep CNN, trained on enough data with enough GPU compute, outperforms classical computer-vision techniques by a wide margin on real-world image classification.

VGGNet

VGGNet's contribution was simplicity and uniformity. Instead of mixing filter sizes, it builds its entire convolutional stage out of small, stacked 3×3 filters, repeated many times, with pooling layers placed at regular intervals. Stacking several small filters back-to-back approximates the receptive field of one larger filter while using fewer parameters. This clean, easy-to-reason-about design made VGG a popular choice as a pretrained backbone for years afterward.

GoogLeNet

GoogLeNet introduced the Inception module (see Inception/Xception below) as its core building block. It let the network go deeper while using fewer parameters than VGGNet. This showed that raw depth wasn't the only lever for better accuracy. Smarter internal wiring mattered too.

ResNet

Stacking more and more convolutional layers eventually stops helping and can even make accuracy worse. Very deep plain networks suffer badly from the vanishing gradient problem: gradients shrink to near-zero as they backpropagate through many layers, so the earliest layers barely get updated at all. ResNet's fix is the residual (skip) connection. Instead of forcing each block of layers to learn a full transformation from scratch, a shortcut carries the block's input forward and adds it directly to the block's output. The block only needs to learn the difference (the residual) from the input. This gives gradients a direct path backward through the shortcut, sidesteps the vanishing-gradient bottleneck, and lets networks with dozens or even hundreds of layers train successfully. It's the same vanishing-gradient failure mode discussed for deep plain networks elsewhere in this track.

Inception / Xception

Rather than committing to one filter size per layer, an Inception module runs several filters of different sizes (say 1×1, 3×3, and 5×5) in parallel on the same input, then concatenates all of their outputs together. This lets the network capture features at multiple spatial scales at once, instead of betting on a single filter size per layer. Xception (Extreme Inception) pushes this idea further with depthwise separable convolutions: it splits a standard convolution into a per-channel spatial pass followed by a 1×1 pass across channels. This achieves similar or better accuracy with fewer parameters and less compute.

Transfer Learning

Why Transfer Learning Exists

Deep learning models are data-hungry and compute-hungry. A CNN that becomes genuinely good at image recognition, such as ResNet, VGG, or InceptionNet, is typically trained on ImageNet, roughly 1.2 million images spread across 1,000 classes, for days or even weeks on powerful GPU clusters. Most people building a real-world image classifier don't have millions of labeled images, weeks of GPU time, or a budget for that kind of compute.

Here's the insight that makes this workable: a model trained on a huge, diverse dataset doesn't merely memorize its 1,000 specific classes. In the process of learning to tell a cat apart from a truck, it's forced to learn general-purpose visual building blocks: edges, curves, textures, shapes, patterns of light and shadow. Those building blocks turn out to be useful for almost any visual task, not only the one the model happened to be trained on. Transfer learning is the practice of taking the knowledge (the learned weights) from solving Problem A and reusing it for Problem B, a different but related problem, instead of training a new model from scratch.

Why not just use the pretrained model as-is?

Its final layer was trained to output probabilities over its original classes, whatever ImageNet's 1,000 categories happen to be. If the actual goal is to classify cat breeds or detect tumors in X-rays, those target classes don't exist in the pretrained model's output layer at all. You can't deploy the model unmodified. You have to adapt it directly.

Anatomy of a CNN: base vs. head

Every CNN built for image classification splits cleanly into two conceptual parts. This split is what makes transfer learning possible:

Convolutional base

The stack of convolutional + pooling layers. Learns what is in the image — hierarchical, increasingly generic-to-specific visual features. Early layers pick up edges, colors, and gradients (extremely generic, useful for nearly any visual task); middle layers pick up textures and simple shapes; the later conv layers pick up more complex, semi-task-specific shapes (eyes, wheels, fur patterns).

Dense / classifier head

The fully connected layers at the end. Learns how to map the extracted features to a specific set of class labels — "these feature combinations mean Persian cat, those mean Siamese cat." This part is entirely tied to the original dataset's specific classes.

Analogy: the conv base is a chef who has mastered universal cooking skills — chopping, dicing, sautéing. The dense head is a recipe card that says "combine these prepped ingredients this exact way to make Dish X." Wanting Dish Y doesn't require re-teaching the chef how to chop — you just need a new recipe card. This is precisely why transfer learning freezes the conv base and replaces the dense head.

The general recipe

  1. Take a pretrained model (e.g. VGG16, ResNet50, MobileNet, InceptionV3), trained on ImageNet.
  2. Remove its original dense/classifier head.
  3. Freeze the convolutional base (make its weights non-trainable) — the expensive, generic feature-learning has already been paid for.
  4. Attach new dense layers on top, ending in an output layer that matches your own number of classes.
  5. Train — but now only the small new head is being trained, which needs far less data and far less compute than training a whole CNN from scratch.
Transfer Learning

Feature Extraction

In feature extraction, the convolutional base is completely frozen — zero training, its weights left exactly as they were after the original ImageNet training. Only the newly added dense layers are trained. The conv base is used purely as a fixed feature extractor: it converts raw images into meaningful numeric feature vectors, and a simple new classifier is trained on top of those vectors.

Analogy: you hire a professional photographer (the frozen conv base) who's already excellent at taking detailed photos of anything — you don't retrain their photography skills. You just hand their photos to a new analyst (the dense head) who has to learn to sort them into your own custom categories.

When to use it
  • Small dataset (a few hundred to a few thousand images)
  • New classes are visually similar to the original dataset (e.g. cat breeds, when the base already saw many animals including cats)
  • Limited compute — training only a small dense head is cheap
  • You want fast results with low overfitting risk (fewer trainable parameters)
Risk if misused

If your images are very different from the original training data (e.g. medical X-rays vs. ImageNet's everyday photos), the frozen features may simply not be relevant enough to your domain, and accuracy suffers.

Transfer Learning

Fine-Tuning

Fine-tuning still starts from a pretrained conv base and a new dense head, but instead of freezing the entire conv base, it unfreezes some of the later (top) convolutional layers and lets them retrain alongside the new dense head. Early layers — which learn very generic features like edges and colors — usually stay frozen, since there's no benefit to retraining something already universal. Later layers, which learned more dataset-specific, complex patterns, are unfrozen and retrained slightly, so they can adapt from "ImageNet-specific shapes" toward "my-dataset-specific shapes."

Why only the top layers, not all of them?

Early layers are generic and useful for virtually every vision task — retraining them risks "catastrophic forgetting," destroying good general knowledge for no benefit. Later layers are more specialized to the original dataset's specific object types, so they're the ones that actually benefit from adapting to a new domain.

Why fine-tuning needs a much lower learning rate

This is the detail that's easiest to get wrong. The pretrained weights are already sitting in a "good neighborhood" of the loss landscape. Fine-tune with a normal-sized learning rate, and the large gradient updates will violently disturb those well-tuned weights, effectively destroying the useful pretrained knowledge — like slamming the brakes hard enough to fly through the windshield. A low learning rate (e.g. 1e-5 instead of a normal 1e-3) makes small, gentle adjustments instead — nudging weights just enough to adapt without erasing what was already learned. RMSprop (or Adam) with a low learning rate is commonly recommended for this phase specifically because it adapts the learning rate per parameter, giving smoother, more stable updates suited to this delicate nudging.

Analogy: fine-tuning is like adjusting a guitar that's already mostly in tune. Turn the pegs very slightly (low learning rate) and it stays in tune, just better. Crank them hard (high learning rate) and you snap a string — undoing all the careful setup that was already there.

Order of operations

  1. Do feature extraction first — freeze the whole conv base, train only the new dense head until it converges reasonably. If the conv base were unfrozen while the dense head still has random, untrained weights, the large, messy gradient signals from that untrained head would backpropagate into the conv base and wreck the pretrained weights.
  2. Once the dense head is reasonably trained, unfreeze the top N conv layers.
  3. Re-compile the model with a very low learning rate.
  4. Continue training (fine-tuning) all unfrozen layers together with the dense head.

Transfer Learning

Code — Both Approaches (Keras / TensorFlow)

Setup — load the pretrained conv base

import tensorflow as tf
from tensorflow.keras.applications import VGG16
from tensorflow.keras import layers, models, optimizers
from tensorflow.keras.preprocessing.image import ImageDataGenerator

IMG_SIZE = (224, 224)
BATCH_SIZE = 32

# load pretrained conv base only — no dense/classifier layers
conv_base = VGG16(
    weights='imagenet',      # load ImageNet-pretrained weights
    include_top=False,       # exclude original dense classifier
    input_shape=(224, 224, 3)
)

Approach A — Feature Extraction: freeze everything, add your own head

conv_base.trainable = False   # freeze the entire convolutional base

model = models.Sequential([
    conv_base,
    layers.GlobalAveragePooling2D(),      # or layers.Flatten()
    layers.Dense(256, activation='relu'),
    layers.Dropout(0.5),
    layers.Dense(num_classes, activation='softmax')  # your own output layer
])

model.compile(
    optimizer=optimizers.RMSprop(learning_rate=1e-3),  # normal LR is fine here
    loss='categorical_crossentropy',
    metrics=['accuracy']
)

model.fit(train_generator, validation_data=val_generator, epochs=10)
# At this stage, only the new Dense layers get trained — the millions of
# VGG16 conv parameters stay completely untouched. This is fast.

Approach B — Fine-Tuning: unfreeze top conv layers, retrain with a low LR

# Step 1: do feature extraction first (as above) until the dense head converges.

# Step 2: unfreeze the base, but only the TOP layers
conv_base.trainable = True

# freeze all layers except the last few (e.g. last 4 layers of VGG16's block5)
fine_tune_at = len(conv_base.layers) - 4

for layer in conv_base.layers[:fine_tune_at]:
    layer.trainable = False       # keep early layers frozen (generic features)
for layer in conv_base.layers[fine_tune_at:]:
    layer.trainable = True        # unfreeze the last few conv layers

# Step 3: recompile with a VERY LOW learning rate — critical step!
model.compile(
    optimizer=optimizers.RMSprop(learning_rate=1e-5),  # much lower than before
    loss='categorical_crossentropy',
    metrics=['accuracy']
)

# Step 4: continue training (fine-tuning)
history_finetune = model.fit(
    train_generator,
    validation_data=val_generator,
    epochs=10
)

Key gotchas

  • Always call compile() again after changing .trainable — Keras won't apply the freeze/unfreeze change until you recompile.
  • Do feature extraction before fine-tuning — never fine-tune from scratch with an untrained head.
  • Use a much smaller learning rate for the fine-tuning phase — typically 10× to 100× smaller than the feature-extraction phase.
  • Watch validation loss closely during fine-tuning — if it starts increasing, you're overfitting or the learning rate is still too high; consider early stopping.

Mental model to remember

  • Conv base = "eyes" — knows how to perceive the world generically.
  • Dense head = "brain" — makes the actual decision/classification.
  • Feature Extraction = reuse the eyes as-is, train a new brain.
  • Fine-Tuning = reuse the eyes mostly as-is, but let them adjust slightly, and train a new brain — used when the visual domain is different enough that the eyes need a bit of retraining too.
  • Low LR in fine-tuning = don't shout instructions at someone who already mostly knows what they're doing — whisper small corrections instead.

5 questions on CNN architectures & transfer learning. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. What specific problem do ResNet's residual (skip) connections solve in very deep networks?

Explanation: Skip connections let a block learn a residual on top of its input rather than a whole transformation from scratch, giving gradients a direct path backward and letting far deeper networks train successfully without early layers stalling out.

2. You have only 400 labeled images of a bird species, visually similar to categories ImageNet already saw plenty of (many bird/animal photos). Per the guidance in this page, which transfer learning approach fits best, and why?

Explanation: Small dataset + visually similar domain is exactly the textbook case for feature extraction: the frozen conv base's generic features already transfer well, and only a small new head needs training, keeping overfitting risk low.

3. Why does fine-tuning specifically require a much lower learning rate (e.g. 1e-5) than a fresh model's normal training (e.g. 1e-3)?

Explanation: A low LR makes small, gentle nudges to weights that already sit in a good region of the loss landscape — a normal-sized LR risks large updates that wreck the pretrained knowledge, the "slamming the brakes so hard you fly through the windshield" scenario.

4. Why must feature extraction be done before fine-tuning, rather than unfreezing conv layers immediately with a brand-new, randomly-initialized dense head?

Explanation: A freshly-initialized dense head produces large, noisy gradients early on. If conv layers are unfrozen at that point, those messy gradients flow back into and disturb the pretrained conv weights — so the head must converge reasonably first, with the conv base still frozen.

5. In fine-tuning, why are the early conv layers kept frozen while only the top (later) conv layers are unfrozen?

Explanation: Early conv layers learn universal, generic visual features that transfer well as-is — retraining them risks catastrophic forgetting. Later layers learned more dataset-specific patterns, which is exactly what benefits from adapting to the new domain.
