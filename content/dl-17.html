17. Attention Mechanism

Alignment scores · softmax weights · a fresh context vector at every decoder step · additive vs multiplicative attention

The previous page ended on a hard limit: a vanilla Seq2Seq encoder-decoder squeezes an entire input sentence — however long, however rich — into a single fixed-size context vector, and the decoder has to generate the whole output using only that one vector. Attention exists to remove exactly that limit. This page builds it up from the problem, through the exact math, to the two competing ways of scoring "relevance" that researchers converged on before Transformers arrived.

Attention

The Problem, One More Time

A long sentence carries a lot of information — many words, many clauses, many relationships between them. Squeezing all of that into one fixed-size vector means detail gets lost or diluted, the same way trying to summarize an entire book chapter in one sentence, and then writing a full book report from only that sentence, throws away almost everything that mattered. Worse, the vanilla architecture puts unfair pressure on both halves of the network at once:

  • The encoder has to anticipate and pack in everything the decoder might ever need — all at once, before the decoder has even started generating anything.
  • The decoder is then stuck reusing that same static summary at every output timestep, even though different output words logically depend on different parts of the input.

Key insight

At any given decoder timestep, you don't need the meaning of the whole input sentence — you need just the relevant subset of words for the specific word you're generating right now.

Example — translating "turn off the lights" into Urdu ("لائٹ بند کرو"). When generating "لائٹ" (lights), the decoder mainly needs "lights." When generating "بند" (off), it mainly needs "off." When generating "کرو" (do/turn), it mainly needs "turn." Forcing the decoder to make all three of those decisions from one single blended summary wastes information and hurts translation quality, especially as sentences get longer. It would be far better if the decoder could dynamically shift its focus across the input at each output step — that's exactly what attention does.

The reading analogy. Think about how you actually read a book: even though the whole page sits in front of your eyes, your attention is only ever on one word or phrase at a time — you don't process the entire page with equal weight simultaneously. You focus on a small relevant chunk, extract its meaning, then move on. Attention gives the decoder exactly this ability: instead of being handed one static summary of the entire input up front, it can look back at the whole input at every single output step and decide, freshly each time, how much weight to give each part of it.

Attention

What Attention Actually Builds

Every encoder timestep produces its own hidden state — h1, h2, h3, h4… one per input word. The vanilla architecture throws away h1, h2, h3 and keeps only the final one. Attention keeps all of them available to the decoder, for the entire decoding process. At each decoder timestep, it computes a weight (usually written α, "alpha") for every encoder hidden state, answering "how relevant is this input word right now?" The context vector for that timestep is then the weighted sum of all the encoder hidden states, dominated by whichever ones got the largest weight:

$$ c_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij} \, h_j $$

Notice the context vector \(c_i\) has the same dimensionality as a single encoder hidden state \(h_j\) — because it's built purely as a weighted sum of vectors that all share that shape. If \(\alpha_{i2}\) (the weight on encoder state \(h_2\)) is much larger than the rest, \(c_i\) will be dominated by \(h_2\)'s information — meaning the decoder is effectively "looking at" input word 2 right now.

This changes what the decoder receives at each timestep. In the vanilla architecture it got two inputs: its own previous hidden/cell state, and the previous output word. Attention adds a third input — the freshly-computed context vector \(c_i\), different at every single decoder timestep, built specifically to highlight whichever encoder states matter most for generating this particular output word. The decoder is no longer relying on one generic, blended summary for the entire sentence; it gets a fresh, targeted summary every step.

Attention

Calculating the Weights, Step by Step

Alpha depends on two things: the encoder's hidden state at a given input position (\(h_j\) — "what that input word means") and the decoder's previous hidden state (\(s_{i-1}\) — "what the decoder has generated/understood so far"). Why does it need \(s_{i-1}\) at all? Because relevance isn't fixed — which input words matter changes at every output step. At decoder step 1, "lights" matters most; at step 2, "off" matters most. The only way the model can know "where the decoder currently is" in generation is by looking at the decoder's own current state. Without \(s_{i-1}\) feeding into the scoring, the attention weights would have no way to shift across timesteps at all — they'd be static, which defeats the entire purpose.

Step 1 — Alignment score (raw, un-normalized relevance)

$$ e_{ij} = a(s_{i-1}, h_j) = V^\top \tanh(W_s s_{i-1} + W_h h_j) $$

Here \(a\) is not a fixed formula like a plain dot product — in the original Bahdanau design, \(a\) is itself a small feedforward neural network (a Dense/fully-connected layer) that takes \(s_{i-1}\) and \(h_j\) as input and outputs a single scalar score. \(W_s\), \(W_h\), and \(V\) are all learned weight matrices/vectors, trained jointly with the rest of the network via backpropagation, exactly like every other weight in the model. The model learns what "relevance" should look like, rather than us hand-defining it.

Step 2 — Normalize with softmax

$$ \alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_k \exp(e_{ik})} $$

This ensures every \(\alpha_{ij}\) for a given decoder timestep \(i\) sums to 1 across the whole input sentence — turning raw scores into a clean "how much attention to pay to each input word" distribution.

Step 3 — Weighted sum → the context vector

$$ c_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij} \, h_j $$

In plain words: alpha is an alignment (similarity) score telling you how strongly a specific encoder timestep matters for the output at a specific decoder timestep — scored by a small trainable network that looks at both "what this input word meant" and "what the decoder currently understands," then normalized so the weights are comparable and sum to 1.

This entire three-step process repeats fresh at every decoder timestep — a new \(c_i\), built from a new set of alphas, computed from that timestep's decoder state. For the "turn off the lights" example: at decoder step 2 (generating "بند" / "off"), the model computes one alignment score per encoder position — how relevant is "turn," "off," "the," "lights" respectively — passes those four scores through softmax, and since "off" is the most relevant word for this specific output, \(\alpha_{22}\) dominates, meaning \(c_2\) ends up built almost entirely from \(h_2\) ("off")'s hidden state.

The context vector doesn't get used alone, either — it's typically concatenated with the decoder's own hidden state, and a learned Dense layer figures out how to combine both pieces before a final softmax over the vocabulary predicts the next word. Concatenation is used rather than blending because \(c_i\) and \(s_i\) represent two genuinely different kinds of information — "what's relevant from the input right now" vs. "what the decoder itself has understood/generated so far" — and concatenation keeps both intact rather than forcing a fixed mix ourselves.

Attention

Two Ways to Score Relevance

Everything above establishes the skeleton — score, softmax, weighted sum — but leaves open exactly how the alignment score \(e_{ij}\) gets computed. Two named variants answer this differently.

Additive Attention (Bahdanau, 2014)

$$ e_{ij} = V^\top \tanh(W_s s_{i-1} + W_h h_j) $$

The decoder's previous state \(s_{i-1}\) and each encoder state \(h_j\) are passed through learned weight matrices, added together, squashed with tanh, and projected to a scalar via a learned vector \(V\). This whole scoring function has its own trainable parameters, separate from the encoder/decoder — hence "additive," since the two inputs are combined by addition inside the network before scoring. Uses \(s_{i-1}\) — the decoder's state before the current word is generated.

Multiplicative Attention (Luong, 2015)

$$ e_{ij} = s_i \cdot h_j $$

Instead of a neural network, Luong attention takes the direct dot product of the decoder's hidden state and each encoder hidden state. Geometrically, a dot product is large when two vectors point in a similar direction — exactly what a similarity score should capture — and it needs zero extra learnable parameters. Uses \(s_i\) — the decoder's current state, the freshest information available, rather than the previous one.

Additive (Bahdanau)Multiplicative (Luong)
Decoder state usedPrevious, \(s_{i-1}\)Current, \(s_i\)
Scoring mechanismSmall feedforward networkDirect dot product
Extra trainable paramsYes (\(W_s, W_h, V\))No (basic "dot" version)
Compute cost / speedHigher / slowerLower / faster
Practical performanceGood, but generally outperformedGenerally better in practice

Why is the simpler option usually the better one? Two reinforcing reasons: fewer parameters to learn means less overfitting risk and faster, more stable convergence — the model isn't spending capacity learning a whole extra scoring network when a dot product already captures similarity well. And using the decoder's current state means the relevance scoring reflects the freshest information available, rather than the state before the current word was generated.

Looking ahead

The Transformer's "Scaled Dot-Product Attention" is a direct descendant of multiplicative (Luong) attention — same dot-product scoring idea, extended into Query/Key/Value matrices and scaled by the square root of the key dimension for numerical stability. That's exactly where the next page picks up.

Attention

One Distinction Worth Naming Early

Everything on this page is technically an inter-sequence form of attention: the Query side comes from the decoder (one sequence), while the Key/Value side comes from the encoder (a different sequence). Once Transformers formalize this same idea with learned Query/Key/Value projections, this pattern gets a name — cross-attention. The next page introduces its counterpart, self-attention — where Query, Key, and Value all come from the same sequence, a word relating to every other word in its own sentence. That single shift — from "two sequences talking to each other" to "one sequence talking to itself" — is the seed that grows into the entire Transformer architecture.
