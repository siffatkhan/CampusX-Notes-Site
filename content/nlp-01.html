What Is Natural Language Processing?

A natural language is a language that evolved naturally in humans through use and repetition, without conscious planning or premeditation — English, Hindi, Mandarin, Spanish, and every other language people actually speak, as opposed to a designed/constructed language like a programming language or Esperanto. Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the field of AI concerned with giving machines the ability to read, understand, interpret, and generate this kind of language.

Why is this hard, and why does it matter so much? Because an enormous share of human knowledge and communication — books, conversations, emails, reviews, tickets, social media — exists as unstructured text, not neat rows and columns. Any system that wants to act intelligently in the real world eventually has to deal with language.

Real-World Applications

  • Contextual / targeted advertisements — ads chosen based on the content of a page or a user's language patterns, not just demographics.
  • Email clients — spam filtering (classifying incoming mail as spam / not spam) and smart reply (auto-suggesting short responses).
  • Social media — removing adult/harmful content (content moderation) and opinion mining on platforms like Twitter (working out what people think about a topic).
  • Search engines — modern search (like Google) increasingly summarizes an answer directly in the results page, so the user often doesn't need to click into a website at all.
  • Chatbots — conversational systems that understand a user's request and respond appropriately.

Common NLP Tasks

NLP is not one algorithm — it's an umbrella over many distinct tasks, each solved with its own techniques (some reused across tasks, some specialized). The rest of this track walks through the building blocks needed to tackle these:

Text / document classification — assigning a category to a whole document (spam vs not-spam, topic tags).
Sentiment analysis — determining whether text expresses a positive, negative, or neutral opinion.
Information retrieval — finding the most relevant documents for a query (the core of search engines).
Part-of-speech (POS) tagging — assigning a grammatical category (noun, verb, adjective, …) to each word.
Language detection & machine translation — identifying what language a text is written in, and converting text from one language to another.
Conversational agents — chatbots and voice assistants that hold a dialogue.
Knowledge graphs & QA systems — structuring facts extracted from text and answering questions over them.
Text summarization — condensing a long document into a short summary.
Topic modeling — discovering the abstract topics running through a collection of documents, commonly using the ML model LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation) — covered in full in chapter 7.
Text generation / autocomplete — the predictive text you see while typing on a keyboard.
Spell checking & grammar correction — detecting and fixing typos and grammatical errors.
Text parsing — analyzing the grammatical structure of a sentence.
Speech-to-text — transcribing spoken audio into written text.

Three Approaches to NLP

Just like the rest of AI, NLP problems can be tackled at three increasing levels of sophistication — from hand-written rules, to models that learn patterns from data, to models that learn both patterns and features automatically.

1. Heuristic / Rule-Based Methods

These are hand-crafted, hacked-together rules devised directly by a human — essentially applying whatever pragmatic trick gets the job done, rather than learning from data. Typical tools:

  • Regular expressions (regex) — let you search, parse, modify, and validate text patterns efficiently (e.g. matching dates, emails, phone numbers).
  • WordNet — a lexical dictionary that gives the relationship between one word and another (e.g. "running" relates to "shoes").
  • "Common sense" fact databases (e.g. Open Mind Common Sense) — stores everyday facts a human would take for granted.

These are quick to build and mostly error-free precisely because a human designed the rules — but they're brittle: they don't generalize to inputs the rule-writer didn't anticipate.

2. Machine Learning Models

Instead of a human writing the rules directly, an ML model looks at data and finds patterns and relationships in it — the "rules" are learned statistically rather than hand-written. Common algorithms used in classic NLP: Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, SVM, LDA, Hidden Markov Models (HMM).

3. Deep Learning Models

DL's big advantage over the previous two approaches: automatic feature generation (no manual feature engineering needed) and, critically, the ability to retain sequential information — the order words appear in, which carries meaning that bag-of-words-style methods throw away.

Challenges in NLP

Language is hard for machines precisely because of the things that make it flexible and expressive for humans. The core recurring difficulties:

Ambiguity — one sentence can have multiple valid readings. "I saw a boy with glasses" could mean the boy was wearing glasses, or that you used glasses (binoculars/spectacles) to see him.
Contextual word meaning — "I ran into the store because we ran out of milk" uses "ran" with two different senses in one sentence.
Colloquialisms & slang — "piece of cake" means "easy" to a human; a machine has no built-in reason to know that.
Synonyms — many different words (car, automobile, vehicle) can mean nearly the same thing, and a system needs to recognize that equivalence.
Irony, sarcasm & tone — "That's just what I needed today" can mean genuine relief or bitter frustration depending entirely on tone/context — the words alone don't disambiguate it.
Spelling errors — typos and misspellings create word forms a model may never have seen.
Creativity — poetry, wordplay, and novel phrasing don't follow predictable statistical patterns.
Language diversity — thousands of languages exist, each with its own grammar, script, and idioms; a technique tuned for English rarely transfers cleanly.

Explanation: Rules designed directly by a human, with no data-driven learning, is exactly the heuristic/rule-based approach — fast to build and mostly error-free since a human designed it, but brittle against inputs the rule-writer didn't foresee.

2. Which deep learning architecture is specifically called out as being used mostly for text generation, distinct from its use in general sequence modeling?

Explanation: Per the notes, RNNs suit general sequential data but struggle with long context, LSTMs retain context/memory better, and GRUs are called out as used mostly for text generation. WordNet is a heuristic lexical dictionary, not a DL architecture.

Explanation: This is the source's own example of ambiguity — a single sentence supporting more than one valid grammatical/semantic reading, distinct from slang (colloquialism), tone (irony/sarcasm), or diversity across languages.

Explanation: This matches the source's real-world application: "search engines like Google summarize what we ask, so we don't have to go inside the website" — a distinct application from ad targeting, spam filtering, or moderation.

Explanation: The notes are explicit: DL's big advantages are automatic feature generation and retaining sequential information; ML models (Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, SVM, LDA, HMM) learn rules from data but don't have those two specific DL advantages.
