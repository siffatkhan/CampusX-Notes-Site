What Is Natural Language Processing?

A natural language is a language that evolved naturally in humans through use and repetition, without conscious planning. English, Hindi, Mandarin, Spanish, and every other language people actually speak fit this description. This is different from a designed or constructed language, like a programming language or Esperanto. Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the field of AI that gives machines the ability to read, understand, interpret, and generate this kind of language.

This matters because most human knowledge and communication exists as unstructured text, not neat rows and columns. Books, conversations, emails, reviews, tickets, and social media posts are all text. Any system that acts intelligently in the real world has to handle language at some point.

Real-World Applications

  • Contextual / targeted advertisements. Ads chosen based on the content of a page or a user's language patterns, not only demographics.
  • Email clients. Spam filtering classifies incoming mail as spam or not spam. Smart reply auto-suggests short responses.
  • Social media. Content moderation removes adult or harmful content. Opinion mining, used on platforms like Twitter, works out what people think about a topic.
  • Search engines. Modern search engines, like Google, increasingly summarize an answer directly on the results page. You often don't need to click into a website at all.
  • Chatbots. Conversational systems that understand a user's request and respond appropriately.

Common NLP Tasks

NLP is not one algorithm. It covers many distinct tasks, each solved with its own techniques. Some techniques get reused across tasks, others stay specialized. The rest of this track walks through the building blocks you need to tackle these tasks:

Text / document classification. Assigns a category to a whole document, like spam vs. not spam, or topic tags.
Sentiment analysis. Determines whether text expresses a positive, negative, or neutral opinion.
Information retrieval. Finds the most relevant documents for a query. This is the core of search engines.
Part-of-speech (POS) tagging. Assigns a grammatical category, like noun, verb, or adjective, to each word.
Language detection & machine translation. Identifies what language a text is written in, and converts text from one language to another.
Conversational agents. Chatbots and voice assistants that hold a dialogue.
Knowledge graphs & QA systems. Structure facts extracted from text and answer questions over them.
Text summarization. Condenses a long document into a short summary.
Topic modeling. Discovers the abstract topics running through a collection of documents, commonly using the ML model LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation). Chapter 7 covers LDA in full.
Text generation / autocomplete. The predictive text you see while typing on a keyboard.
Spell checking & grammar correction. Detects and fixes typos and grammatical errors.
Text parsing. Analyzes the grammatical structure of a sentence.
Speech-to-text. Transcribes spoken audio into written text.

Three Approaches to NLP

Like the rest of AI, you can tackle NLP problems at three increasing levels of sophistication, from hand-written rules, to models that learn patterns from data, to models that learn both patterns and features automatically.

1. Heuristic / Rule-Based Methods

These are hand-crafted rules a human writes directly. The rule-writer applies whatever practical trick gets the job done, instead of learning from data. Typical tools:

  • Regular expressions (regex). These let you search, parse, modify, and validate text patterns efficiently, for example matching dates, emails, and phone numbers.
  • WordNet. A lexical dictionary that gives the relationship between one word and another. For example, "running" relates to "shoes".
  • "Common sense" fact databases, like Open Mind Common Sense, store everyday facts a human would take for granted.

These are quick to build and mostly error-free because a human designed the rules. But they're brittle. They don't generalize to inputs the rule-writer didn't anticipate.

2. Machine Learning Models

Instead of a human writing the rules directly, an ML model looks at data and finds patterns and relationships in it. The model learns the "rules" statistically, rather than by hand. Common algorithms used in classic NLP include Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, SVM, LDA, and Hidden Markov Models (HMM).

3. Deep Learning Models

Deep learning's big advantage over the previous two approaches is automatic feature generation, so you don't need manual feature engineering. It also retains sequential information, the order words appear in, which carries meaning that bag-of-words-style methods throw away.

Challenges in NLP

Language is hard for machines because of the same things that make it flexible and expressive for humans. The core recurring difficulties:

Ambiguity. One sentence has multiple valid readings. "I saw a boy with glasses" means either the boy was wearing glasses, or you used glasses, like binoculars or spectacles, to see him.
Contextual word meaning. "I ran into the store because we ran out of milk" uses "ran" with two different senses in one sentence.
Colloquialisms & slang. "Piece of cake" means "easy" to a human. A machine has no built-in reason to know that.
Synonyms. Many different words, like car, automobile, and vehicle, mean nearly the same thing. A system needs to recognize that equivalence.
Irony, sarcasm & tone. "That's exactly what I needed today" means genuine relief or bitter frustration, depending entirely on tone and context. The words alone don't disambiguate it.
Spelling errors. Typos and misspellings create word forms a model has never seen before.
Creativity. Poetry, wordplay, and novel phrasing don't follow predictable statistical patterns.
Language diversity. Thousands of languages exist, each with its own grammar, script, and idioms. A technique tuned for English rarely transfers cleanly.

1. A team builds a spam filter using hand-written regex rules ("contains 'FREE MONEY' → spam") designed entirely by an engineer, with no training data involved. Which approach to NLP is this?

Explanation: Rules designed directly by a human, with no data-driven learning, is exactly the heuristic/rule-based approach. It's fast to build and mostly error-free since a human designed it, but brittle against inputs the rule-writer didn't foresee.

2. Which deep learning architecture is specifically called out as being used mostly for text generation, distinct from its use in general sequence modeling?

Explanation: The notes explain that RNNs suit general sequential data but struggle with long context, LSTMs retain context and memory better, and GRUs are called out as used mostly for text generation. WordNet is a heuristic lexical dictionary, not a DL architecture.

3. "I saw a boy with glasses" having multiple valid readings (the boy wore glasses, vs. you used glasses to see him) illustrates which NLP challenge?

Explanation: This is the source's own example of ambiguity. It's a single sentence supporting more than one valid grammatical or semantic reading, distinct from slang (colloquialism), tone (irony or sarcasm), or diversity across languages.

4. A search engine that reads a user's query, finds a matching news article, and writes a short summary directly on the results page (so the user never clicks through) is demonstrating which application area?

Explanation: This matches the source's real-world application: "search engines like Google summarize what we ask, so we don't have to go inside the website." This is a distinct application from ad targeting, spam filtering, or moderation.

5. Which statement correctly distinguishes ML models from DL models in NLP, per the source notes?

Explanation: The notes are explicit: DL's big advantages are automatic feature generation and retaining sequential information. ML models (Naive Bayes, Logistic Regression, SVM, LDA, HMM) learn rules from data but don't have those two specific DL advantages.
