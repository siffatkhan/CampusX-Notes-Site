Bias Variance Trade Off

Bias

The inability of an ML model to truly capture the relationship in the training data — such a model has high bias. Bias = error due to wrong assumptions in the model.

  • Model is too simple
  • Misses the real pattern in the training data
  • Makes the same mistake on most datasets

Example: you fit a straight line to curved data — the model cannot follow the shape. Result: high training error and high test error.

Variance

The difference in fit across different datasets. Example: training error = 10, test error = 100 → gap = 90 → variance is high.

  • Model is too sensitive
  • Learns noise
  • Changes a lot if the data changes

Result: low training error, high test error.

Intuition: high bias → model too rigid. High variance → model too flexible. Goal: balance both.

Techniques to Handle the Tradeoff

1. Regularization

Penalize large coefficients. Effect: reduces model complexity and controls overfitting — the model is forced to stay smooth. Used in both linear regression and logistic regression.

There are 3 kinds of regularization technique: Ridge (L2), Lasso (L1), and Elastic Net (a combination of both).

In an overfit model, the slope (m) value would be extremely high; a lower m value represents underfitting. A lesser slope means x plays less of a role in determining output y; a high slope m means x plays a greater role. To reduce overfitting, we have to reduce the slope m — so we add a regularization term to the loss function that penalizes large coefficients, and our goal becomes minimizing that combined magnitude.

Lambda (λ) is a hyperparameter controlling how strongly coefficients are penalized — it can be tuned lower (less regularization, closer to the unregularized model) or higher (more regularization, more shrinkage toward zero).

2. Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating)

Train many models on different random samples of the data and average their predictions. Effect: reduces variance. Example: Random Forest.

3. Boosting

Train models sequentially, where each new model focuses on the previous models' mistakes. Effect: reduces bias and improves overall accuracy. Examples: AdaBoost, Gradient Boosting.

Test Your Understanding

Hard

5 questions on Day 62. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. A model shows training error = 8 and test error = 9 (a small gap), but both are high. Which problem does this indicate?

Explanation: A small train/test gap rules out high variance (which needs a large gap, e.g. 10 vs 100). Both errors being high — with the model making the same mistake consistently — is the signature of high bias / underfitting.

2. Which technique reduces variance by training many models on different random samples and averaging their predictions?

Explanation: Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) trains many models on random samples and averages predictions, reducing variance — Random Forest is the canonical example. Boosting trains sequentially to reduce bias instead.

3. You increase λ (lambda) sharply in a Ridge Regression. What happens to the coefficient magnitudes and where does the model move on the bias-variance spectrum?

Explanation: Per the notes: an overfit model has extremely high m (slope); lower m represents underfitting/simplicity. Regularization's job is to reduce slope m — so as λ increases, coefficients shrink, complexity drops, and the model trades variance for bias.

4. Using the slider widget on this page: at the far-left (complexity = 1) position, which curve is highest and what regime does that represent?

Explanation: At minimum complexity the model is too simple to capture the pattern — bias² is high while variance is low, which is the underfitting end of the tradeoff curve.

5. Why does Boosting reduce bias rather than variance, unlike Bagging?

Explanation: Boosting's defining mechanic is sequential training where each model focuses on what previous ones got wrong — directly attacking systematic error (bias), unlike bagging's parallel averaging, which smooths out variance instead.
