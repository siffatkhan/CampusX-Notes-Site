28. Support Vector Machines (SVM)

Maximum-margin hyperplanes · support vectors · hard vs soft margin · the kernel trick

Extended notes — this topic isn't covered in the CampusX playlist notes this site is based on. Written from scratch to continue the curriculum, in the same style as the rest of the site.

Logistic regression finds a line that separates two classes. SVM asks a sharper question: among all the lines that separate the classes, which one is safest — the one with the most breathing room on either side? That single idea — maximize the margin, not just achieve separation — is the whole of SVM's geometric intuition, and it turns out to generalize into one of the most powerful classical classifiers ever devised.

Classification

Geometric Intuition

Imagine two clusters of points, cleanly separable by a straight line. There are infinitely many lines that would separate them perfectly — a line can wobble quite a bit and still keep every point on the correct side. SVM picks the one specific line that sits as far as possible from both classes at once — maximizing the width of the empty "street" between them. This street is called the margin, and the boundary line running down its middle is the maximum-margin hyperplane.

The points that end up sitting exactly on the edges of that street — the ones closest to the boundary, "holding up" the margin on either side — are called support vectors. They are the only points that matter to the final model: if you deleted every other training point and re-fit, the boundary wouldn't move at all, because it was never determined by the bulk of the data — only by the handful of closest, most difficult-to-separate points on each side.

Why maximize the margin at all?

A wide margin means the boundary sits with a comfortable buffer between the two classes — new, slightly-off-distribution test points are less likely to fall on the wrong side. A boundary squeezed right up against the training points has essentially no room for error on new data. This is a generalization argument, not just an aesthetic one.

The Math — Margin Width and the Optimization Objective

Write the separating hyperplane as \(w^\top x + b = 0\), same linear form as logistic regression's decision boundary. The two dashed margin lines in the diagram above are \(w^\top x + b = 1\) and \(w^\top x + b = -1\) — offset by exactly 1 in either direction. Some geometry (the perpendicular distance between two parallel hyperplanes) shows the width of the street between them is:

$$ \text{margin width} = \frac{2}{\|w\|} $$

This is the key insight that turns "maximize the margin" into a solvable optimization problem: margin width is inversely proportional to \(\|w\|\) (the length of the weight vector). Maximizing \( \frac{2}{\|w\|} \) is the same problem as minimizing \(\|w\|\) — or, for mathematical convenience (it removes an awkward square root during optimization), minimizing \(\|w\|^2\) instead.

$$ \min_{w,b} \; \|w\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(w^\top x_i + b) \geq 1 \;\; \text{for every training point } i $$

Read the constraint carefully: \(y_i \in \{-1, +1\}\) is the true label, and \(w^\top x_i + b\) is the raw score for point \(i\). If a point is correctly classified with score on the correct side of its margin line, \(y_i(w^\top x_i + b)\) comes out \(\geq 1\). So the constraint says, in plain English: every single training point must be correctly classified, and moreover must sit at or beyond its margin line — nothing is allowed to creep inside the street. Combined with the objective, the full problem reads: make the margin as wide as possible, without letting any point violate it.

Note. The full derivation solves this via Lagrange multipliers, producing a "dual" problem expressed entirely in terms of dot products between training points — which is precisely what makes the kernel trick (below) possible. That derivation is out of scope here; what matters is the objective above and what the dual form enables.

Hard Margin vs Soft Margin

The formulation above — every point must sit strictly outside the margin, no exceptions — is called a hard margin. It only works when the data is perfectly linearly separable. Two problems make this fragile in the real world: real data almost never separates perfectly (there's noise, overlap, mislabeled points), and even when it technically does, a single outlier sitting close to the other class can force the margin to shrink dramatically or make the problem unsolvable entirely — one weird point ruins everything.

The fix is a soft margin: allow some points to violate the margin (or even end up on the wrong side entirely), but add a penalty for doing so. The optimization becomes a trade-off between two competing goals — wide margin vs. few/small violations — controlled by a hyperparameter, C.

High C

Misclassifications are heavily penalized. The model tries hard to classify every training point correctly, even if that means a narrower margin that hugs the data tightly. Behaves close to a hard margin. Risk: overfitting to noise/outliers, high variance.

Low C

Misclassifications are cheap. The model prioritizes a wide, smooth margin and is willing to tolerate several points on the wrong side of it (even a few misclassifications) to get there. Risk: underfitting if C is too small.

This is, again, exactly the bias-variance dial from Section 21 — C for SVM plays the same role as C did for logistic regression's regularization in Section 25 (unsurprisingly: it's tuned the same way, via cross-validation over a range of values, typically on a log scale like [0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100]).

The Kernel Trick — Geometric Intuition & Code

Everything above assumes the data is separable by a straight line (or flat hyperplane). Real data frequently isn't — the classic example is one class forming a disc, completely surrounded by a ring of the other class (the same dataset used in Section 25's polynomial features example). No straight line in 2D separates a disc from the ring around it.

The "lift it into 3D" intuition

Picture the same disc-and-ring data, but now imagine lifting every point upward by an amount proportional to its distance from the center — so the central disc points barely rise, while the ring points (farther from center) rise a lot. Viewed in this new 3D space (x, y, height), the disc points now form a low cluster and the ring points form a raised cluster above them — and a single flat plane slid in between separates them perfectly. Flatten that plane back down into the original 2D view, and it appears as a circle — a decision boundary a straight line could never draw, achieved by a straight (flat) boundary in a higher-dimensional space.

This is precisely Section 25's polynomial-features move, generalized. The kernel trick's genius is a computational shortcut: recall from "The Math" above that the SVM's dual optimization problem only ever needs the dot products between pairs of points, never the raw coordinates directly. A kernel function \(K(x_i, x_j)\) computes what the dot product would be if you had transformed both points into some higher-dimensional space first — without ever actually performing that transformation or even knowing what that space looks like. You get all the benefit of the higher-dimensional separating hyperplane, at the computational cost of working in the original, low-dimensional space.

Common kernels

KernelBoundary shapeWhen to use
linearStraight line / flat hyperplaneData is already (roughly) linearly separable; also the fastest, and preferred for very high-dimensional data (e.g. text) where extra curvature rarely helps
polyPolynomial curvesModerate, structured non-linearity; degree hyperparameter controls curve flexibility
rbf (Radial Basis Function / Gaussian)Smooth, arbitrarily flexible closed curvesThe default general-purpose choice for non-linear data — including the disc-in-a-ring case

RBF's gamma hyperparameter

The RBF kernel measures similarity between two points as a function of the distance between them, decaying smoothly to zero as they get farther apart. gamma controls how quickly that similarity decays — equivalently, how much "reach" a single training point has.

Low gamma — wide-reaching influence per point, smoother/simpler boundary. Too low → underfitting.
High gamma — each point's influence shrinks to a tight neighborhood around itself, letting the boundary wrap tightly around individual points. Too high → overfitting (boundary that memorizes training noise, similar in spirit to KNN with a very small K).

Code — kernel choice with sklearn's SVC

from sklearn.svm import SVC
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.datasets import make_circles

X, y = make_circles(n_samples=500, noise=0.05, factor=0.4, random_state=42)

# a linear kernel cannot separate a disc from a surrounding ring
linear_model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC(kernel='linear', C=1.0))
linear_model.fit(X, y)
print("linear kernel accuracy:", linear_model.score(X, y))   # ~50%, no better than guessing

# RBF kernel implicitly lifts the data into a space where it IS linearly
# separable, without ever explicitly computing that transformation
rbf_model = make_pipeline(
    StandardScaler(),
    SVC(kernel='rbf', C=1.0, gamma='scale')   # 'scale' = sensible default, 1/(n_features * X.var())
)
rbf_model.fit(X, y)
print("rbf kernel accuracy:", rbf_model.score(X, y))   # near-perfect

# tune both C and gamma jointly — they interact
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV
param_grid = {'svc__C': [0.1, 1, 10, 100], 'svc__gamma': [0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1]}
grid = GridSearchCV(rbf_model, param_grid, cv=5)
grid.fit(X, y)
print(grid.best_params_)
SVM for regression. The same margin idea flips into SVR (Support Vector Regression): instead of maximizing a margin that keeps classes apart, SVR fits a "tube" of width \(\epsilon\) around the regression line and only penalizes points that fall outside that tube — points inside are considered "close enough" and cost nothing. Same kernel trick, same C, same underlying machinery — sklearn exposes it as sklearn.svm.SVR.

Test Your Understanding

Hard

5 questions on SVM. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. What defines a "support vector"?

Explanation: Support vectors are the points closest to the boundary — the hardest to classify — and are the only points that actually determine where the maximum-margin hyperplane sits. Removing any non-support-vector point and re-fitting leaves the boundary unchanged.

2. Margin width is 2/||w||. If training pushes ||w|| to be larger, what happens to the margin, and what is the SVM objective doing about it?

Explanation: Margin width is inversely proportional to ||w||, so a bigger ||w|| means a narrower margin. That's exactly why the objective minimizes ||w||^2 subject to the classification constraints — minimizing ||w|| is mathematically identical to maximizing the margin 2/||w||.

3. Your training data has one clear outlier sitting deep inside the opposite class's cluster. With a hard margin SVM, what happens?

Explanation: Hard margin requires every point to satisfy the margin constraint with zero exceptions. A single deeply-embedded outlier can make the constraints infeasible, or force an extremely narrow margin around it — exactly the fragility that motivates the soft margin's tolerance for some violations.

4. What computational shortcut does the kernel trick actually exploit?

Explanation: The dual SVM formulation depends on the data only through pairwise dot products. Kernel functions substitute in the dot product that would result from an implicit, often much higher-dimensional, feature transformation — getting the benefit of that transformation at the cost of computing in the original space.

5. You set gamma extremely high on an RBF-kernel SVC and observe near-perfect training accuracy but poor test accuracy. What's the most likely explanation?

Explanation: High gamma shrinks each point's zone of influence in the RBF kernel, allowing the decision boundary to curve tightly around individual points — memorizing the training set (high variance) rather than learning a generalizable shape. Lowering gamma (or C) would counteract this.
