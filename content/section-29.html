29. Decision Trees

Entropy, Gini & Information Gain · overfitting via depth · Regression Trees · visualizing trees with dtreeviz

Extended notes — this topic isn't covered in the CampusX playlist notes this site is based on. Written from scratch to continue the curriculum, in the same style as the rest of the site.

A decision tree makes predictions the way a person plays "20 questions": ask a yes/no (or threshold) question about a feature, follow the answer down a branch, ask another question, and repeat until you land on an answer. It's arguably the most human-readable model on this site — you can literally trace the exact chain of if/else reasoning behind any single prediction — and it forms the foundation for the ensemble methods (Random Forest, Bagging) covered in Sections 31–32.

Classification & Regression

Geometric Intuition

Where logistic regression and SVM draw a single boundary line (possibly curved, with polynomial features or kernels) across the whole feature space, a decision tree carves the space into axis-aligned rectangular regions, one split at a time. Every internal node asks a single-feature question like "is age < 30?" — geometrically, a vertical or horizontal cut through the feature space. Stack enough of these cuts and you can approximate almost any boundary shape, blocky and rectangular rather than smooth, but arbitrarily fine-grained.

Illustrative aid — generated for this site.

Each box is a node; the boxes with no children are leaves, and each leaf holds the final prediction — a class label (classification) or a numeric value (regression). Building a tree means repeatedly asking: of every possible question I could ask about every feature, which one best separates the classes (or reduces the variance) at this node? That "which question is best" decision is what entropy, Gini impurity, and information gain formalize.

Classification

Entropy, Gini Impurity & Information Gain

A tree needs some numeric way to score "how good is this candidate split?" Every criterion below measures the same underlying idea — impurity, or how mixed-up the classes are within a group of samples — just with different formulas.

Entropy — disorder, borrowed from information theory

$$ H = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \log_2(p_k) $$

Coin-flip analogy

Think of entropy as "how surprised are you, on average, by the outcome?" A coin that always lands heads (p=1.0) surprises you never — entropy = 0, zero uncertainty. A perfectly fair coin (p=0.5 heads, p=0.5 tails) is maximally unpredictable — entropy is at its peak, 1 bit. A group of samples works the same way: a leaf that's 100% one class has entropy 0 (totally "pure," no surprise about what class a random sample from it would be); a leaf split 50/50 between two classes has maximum entropy (a coin flip — you genuinely can't guess).

Gini Impurity — the faster, more common default

$$ G = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k^2 $$

Gini measures roughly the same thing as entropy — how likely you'd be to randomly mislabel a sample if you assigned it a label by randomly guessing according to the class proportions in that node — but avoids the logarithm, making it noticeably cheaper to compute across thousands of candidate splits during training. In practice, entropy and Gini almost always pick the same or near-identical splits; Gini is scikit-learn's default (criterion='gini') purely for speed.

Information Gain — the split-selection rule

Neither entropy nor Gini alone tells you which feature/threshold to split on — you need to compare a node's impurity before a split against the (weighted average) impurity of the children after the split. That reduction is the information gain:

$$ \text{Gain} = H(\text{parent}) - \sum_{\text{children } c} \frac{n_c}{n_{\text{parent}}} H(c) $$

At every node, a decision tree tries every possible feature and every possible threshold, computes the information gain each candidate split would produce, and greedily picks the single split with the highest gain — the one that does the most to separate the classes. This repeats recursively at every new child node.

Worked example — one small split, by hand

Suppose a node has 10 samples: 6 labeled "Yes" (will buy) and 4 labeled "No." A candidate split on "income < 50k" divides them into a left child of 5 samples (4 Yes, 1 No) and a right child of 5 samples (2 Yes, 3 No).

GroupCompositionEntropyGini
Parent6 Yes, 4 No (p=0.6/0.4)\(-0.6\log_2 0.6 - 0.4\log_2 0.4 \approx 0.971\)\(1-(0.6^2+0.4^2) = 0.48\)
Left child4 Yes, 1 No (p=0.8/0.2)\(-0.8\log_2 0.8-0.2\log_2 0.2 \approx 0.722\)\(1-(0.8^2+0.2^2)=0.32\)
Right child2 Yes, 3 No (p=0.4/0.6)\(-0.4\log_2 0.4-0.6\log_2 0.6 \approx 0.971\)\(1-(0.4^2+0.6^2)=0.48\)

Weighted child entropy: \( \frac{5}{10}(0.722) + \frac{5}{10}(0.971) = 0.847 \). Information gain = \(0.971 - 0.847 = 0.124\) bits. The tree would compare this gain against every other candidate split (other thresholds on income, splits on other features entirely) and keep whichever produces the largest gain. Note the right child ended up with the exact same 0.971 entropy as the parent — this particular split did very little to purify that branch, which is exactly what a low information gain is telling you.

Classification & Regression

Hyperparameters & Overfitting/Underfitting in Decision Trees

Left completely unconstrained, a decision tree will keep splitting until every single leaf is 100% pure — often meaning leaves with just one training sample in them. This achieves perfect training accuracy and is one of the purest, most textbook examples of overfitting on this whole site: the tree has essentially memorized the training set's noise, sample by sample, rather than learning a generalizable pattern. This is Section 21's bias-variance tradeoff at its most extreme — a fully grown tree is close to zero bias, maximal variance.

The fix is to constrain how much the tree is allowed to grow, via hyperparameters:

HyperparameterWhat it limitsEffect of a smaller value
max_depthHow many splits deep the tree can go, root to leafSimpler, shallower tree — higher bias, lower variance
min_samples_splitMinimum samples a node must have before it's allowed to split furtherLarger values prevent splitting on tiny, noise-driven subgroups
min_samples_leafMinimum samples allowed to remain in any leafLarger values forbid ultra-specific single-sample leaves
max_featuresHow many features are considered at each splitFewer features per split adds randomness/diversity — mainly a lever for Random Forests (Section 31), less commonly tuned on a single standalone tree

Too unconstrained → overfitting

max_depth=None, min_samples_leaf=1: the tree chases every quirk in the training data. Great training score, poor test score.

Too constrained → underfitting

max_depth=1 (a "decision stump"): the tree can barely separate anything more than the single most obvious split. Both training and test scores suffer.

As always, the right values are found empirically — sweep max_depth over a range with cross-validation and watch for the same U-shaped validation-error curve seen with KNN's K and polynomial degree.

Code — constraining tree growth

from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

# unconstrained -- will overfit on anything but trivial data
overfit_tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=42)
overfit_tree.fit(X_train, y_train)
print("train:", overfit_tree.score(X_train, y_train))   # often 1.0
print("test:", overfit_tree.score(X_test, y_test))       # noticeably lower

# constrained -- generalizes better
tuned_tree = DecisionTreeClassifier(
    criterion='gini',
    max_depth=5,
    min_samples_split=10,
    min_samples_leaf=5,
    random_state=42
)
tuned_tree.fit(X_train, y_train)
print("train:", tuned_tree.score(X_train, y_train))
print("test:", tuned_tree.score(X_test, y_test))          # smaller train/test gap
Regression

Regression Trees

Everything about tree structure carries over unchanged for predicting numbers instead of categories — same recursive splitting, same idea of carving the feature space into regions. Only two things change.

Split criterion: variance reduction, not entropy/Gini. Entropy and Gini only make sense for categorical class proportions. For a numeric target, the tree instead picks the split that most reduces the variance of the target values within each resulting child — equivalently, minimizes the (weighted) mean squared error of predicting each child's mean for its own members.
Leaf prediction: the mean, not the majority class. A classification leaf predicts whichever class is most common among its samples. A regression leaf predicts the average target value of the samples that landed in it — every point falling into the same leaf gets the exact same predicted number, which is why regression tree predictions look like a "staircase" of flat, constant segments rather than a smooth curve.

Code — DecisionTreeRegressor

from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeRegressor

reg_tree = DecisionTreeRegressor(
    criterion='squared_error',   # variance-reduction split criterion
    max_depth=4,
    min_samples_leaf=10,
    random_state=42
)
reg_tree.fit(X_train, y_train)
y_pred = reg_tree.predict(X_test)

# every sample landing in the same leaf gets an IDENTICAL prediction —
# plotting predictions vs. a single input feature shows a blocky "staircase",
# unlike linear regression's single smooth sloped line
Practical

Decision Tree Visualization with dtreeviz

Scikit-learn's built-in plot_tree draws the same kind of box-and-branch diagram shown earlier on this page — useful, but every node is just a plain box of text (the split rule, the impurity score, the class counts). It doesn't show you how the data is actually distributed at each split, just summary numbers.

dtreeviz is a third-party library that renders each node as an actual colored distribution plot — at a classification split, you see overlapping histograms of each class's feature values with the chosen threshold drawn as a vertical line directly through them, so you can visually see why that particular threshold was chosen (it sits right where the two classes' distributions start to separate). Leaf nodes are rendered as pie charts or bars showing the exact class mix, instead of a plain "class = X" label. For regression trees, it shows scatter plots of the target values at each node with the split threshold overlaid.

Why this matters

plot_tree tells you what the tree decided. dtreeviz tells you why — you can see the actual shape of the data that led to each cut, which is invaluable for explaining a model's decisions to a non-technical stakeholder, or for sanity-checking whether a split looks like real signal versus a threshold squeezed between two nearly-identical noisy clusters.

Code — plain plot_tree vs dtreeviz

# --- sklearn's built-in visualization: plain boxes ---
from sklearn.tree import plot_tree
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

plt.figure(figsize=(12, 8))
plot_tree(tuned_tree, feature_names=X_train.columns,
          class_names=['No', 'Yes'], filled=True)
plt.show()
# shows: split rule, gini, sample counts, class counts -- as plain text in a box

# --- dtreeviz: colored distributions at every split ---
# pip install dtreeviz
from dtreeviz.trees import dtreeviz

viz = dtreeviz(
    tuned_tree, X_train, y_train,
    target_name='approved',
    feature_names=list(X_train.columns),
    class_names=['No', 'Yes']
)
viz.save('tree_visualization.svg')
# shows: overlapping histograms of each class's feature distribution
# at every split, with the exact threshold drawn through them --
# you can literally see the two class distributions being cut apart

Test Your Understanding

Hard

5 questions on Decision Trees. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. A leaf node contains 40 samples, all of the same class. What is its entropy and Gini impurity?

Explanation: With p=1 for the single present class, entropy = -1*log2(1) = 0, and Gini = 1 - 1^2 = 0. A node with only one class is "pure" — zero uncertainty about a randomly drawn sample's class, zero impurity by either measure.

2. Why does scikit-learn use Gini impurity as its default splitting criterion instead of entropy?

Explanation: Both entropy and Gini measure impurity and generally agree on which split is best. Gini's formula (1 - sum of squared probabilities) has no logarithm, making it cheaper to compute across the huge number of candidate splits a tree evaluates — pure computational efficiency, not an accuracy advantage.

3. A decision tree is trained with max_depth=None and min_samples_leaf=1, achieving 100% training accuracy but poor test accuracy. What is the fix most directly targeting this problem?

Explanation: 100% train accuracy with much worse test accuracy is the classic overfitting signature of an unconstrained tree. Constraining depth and leaf/split sample minimums directly limits the tree's capacity to memorize noise, trading a bit of bias for a large reduction in variance.

4. How does a regression tree decide what value to predict at a given leaf, and what split criterion produced that leaf?

Explanation: Regression trees swap entropy/Gini (which need categorical class proportions) for variance reduction as the split criterion, and predict the mean of each leaf's target values rather than a majority class — since there's no "class" to vote on, only numbers to average.

5. What does dtreeviz show at each split that sklearn's plain plot_tree does not?

Explanation: plot_tree only shows summary text (rule, impurity, counts) in a plain box. dtreeviz renders the actual per-class distribution of the splitting feature at each node with the threshold overlaid, letting you visually verify why that cut point was chosen — information plot_tree's boxes simply don't contain.
