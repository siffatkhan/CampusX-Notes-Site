9. Activation Functions

Why non-linearity is required · the 5 properties of an ideal activation function · Sigmoid, Tanh, ReLU · Day 23's ReLU variants

Day 22

Why Activation Functions Are Needed

In an artificial neural network, each neuron forms a weighted sum of its inputs — \( z = w \cdot x + b \) — and then passes that scalar value through a function called the activation function (or transfer function), giving \( a = g(z) \). This function decides whether, and how strongly, that neuron "fires" — whether it gets activated.

Why this can't just be skipped: if \( g \) is the identity (or any linear function), then no matter how many layers are stacked, the entire network collapses mathematically into a single linear function of the input. A composition of linear functions is still linear: \( g_2(g_1(x)) = w_2(w_1 x + b_1) + b_2 = (w_2 w_1)x + (w_2 b_1 + b_2) \) — which is just another line. Ten layers without non-linear activations behave exactly like one layer of plain linear regression, no matter how many neurons or layers you add. Non-linear activation functions are what let a deep network actually use its depth — to represent curved decision boundaries, and to solve classification problems that aren't linearly separable.
Day 22

Five Properties of an Ideal Activation Function

1. Non-linear

As shown above, this is non-negotiable — it's the only reason stacking layers adds representational power at all.

2. Differentiable

Backpropagation needs \( \partial a/\partial z \) at every neuron to run the chain rule. A function with no defined derivative can't have its gradient computed, so gradient descent can't use it directly.

3. Computationally inexpensive

This function runs once per neuron, per forward pass, per training example — in a large network that's billions of evaluations. Cheap functions (a max, a threshold) beat expensive ones (exponentials) purely on training speed.

4. Zero-centered

Zero-centered means the function's outputs are distributed around zero (roughly as many negative as positive), so the next layer's inputs are naturally normalized rather than uniformly positive or uniformly negative. Networks converge faster when the values flowing through them are centered this way — tanh has this property, sigmoid does not.

5. Non-saturating

A "saturating" function squeezes a wide range of inputs into a narrow output range — sigmoid squeezes all of \( (-\infty, \infty) \) into (0, 1). Far from the origin, the curve goes nearly flat, so its derivative goes nearly to zero — this is precisely the mechanism behind the vanishing gradient problem from chapter 7. A non-saturating function like ReLU keeps a real, non-vanishing derivative across its active region.

No single common activation function satisfies all five perfectly — choosing one is always a trade-off, which is exactly why the field has produced so many variants.

Day 22

Sigmoid Activation Function

$$ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} \,,\qquad \text{range } [0, 1] $$

Sigmoid squashes any real number into the range [0, 1], which conveniently reads as a probability — the reason it's still standard in the output layer of binary classifiers. It's non-linear and differentiable, so it satisfies those two requirements.

Disadvantages

  • Saturating — flattens for large |z|, directly causing vanishing gradients in deep networks (chapter 7).
  • Not zero-centered — outputs are always positive (0 to 1), never negative, which slows and complicates convergence for the next layer.
  • Computationally expensive — involves an exponential, evaluated at every neuron on every forward pass.

Where it's used today

Essentially nobody uses sigmoid in hidden layers anymore — ReLU and its variants dominate there. Sigmoid survives almost exclusively in the output layer for binary classification, where its [0,1] range as a probability is exactly what's wanted, and there's only one layer's worth of saturation risk rather than a whole deep stack of it.

Day 22

Tanh (Hyperbolic Tangent) Activation Function

$$ \tanh(z) = \frac{e^{z}-e^{-z}}{e^{z}+e^{-z}} \,,\qquad \text{range } [-1, 1] $$

Tanh is really a rescaled, shifted sigmoid — it's non-linear and differentiable just the same, but its range is [-1, 1] instead of [0, 1]. That shift matters: because tanh's output can be negative, it is zero-centered, which sigmoid is not — one of the five ideal properties that tanh satisfies and sigmoid doesn't, and the reason tanh was historically preferred over sigmoid for hidden layers.

Tanh still has the same core weakness as sigmoid, though: it's still a saturating function — flat and near-zero-derivative for large |z| — and it's still computationally slower than a simple max operation. Being zero-centered helps convergence, but doesn't rescue it from vanishing gradients in deep networks, which is why ReLU eventually displaced both sigmoid and tanh as the default hidden-layer choice.

Day 22

ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit)

$$ \text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z) $$

ReLU is almost embarrassingly simple: negative inputs become exactly zero, positive inputs pass through completely unchanged. That simplicity is the whole point.

Advantages

  • Non-linear (still satisfies the core requirement)
  • Non-saturating in the positive region — the derivative is exactly 1 for any positive input, no matter how large, so it never flattens out.
  • Computationally inexpensive — a single comparison, no exponentials.
  • Faster convergence in deep networks in practice, largely because it avoids the saturation-driven vanishing gradients that plague sigmoid/tanh.

Disadvantages

  • Not fully differentiable — there's a sharp corner exactly at \( z=0 \) (same corner issue MAE and the L1 penalty have); in practice frameworks just define the derivative at 0 as 0 or 1 by convention and move on.
  • Not zero-centered — outputs are always ≥ 0, never negative. This is addressed in practice with batch normalization (chapter 10), which re-centers activations between layers.
  • Dying ReLU — covered in full detail next.
Day 23

ReLU Variants Explained

The dying ReLU problem occurs when a neuron gets stuck permanently outputting zero: since \( \max(0,z) \) has a derivative of exactly 0 for any negative \( z \), once a neuron's weighted input consistently falls below zero, its gradient is always zero, so gradient descent can never update its weights again — the neuron is "dead" for good. If more than roughly 50% of a layer's neurons die this way, the network loses enough capacity that it can no longer find useful patterns in the data.

Why dying ReLU happens

  • A large negative gradient step pushes a neuron's weights so far that its weighted input becomes negative for essentially every training example it will ever see.
  • High learning rate — big weight updates are more likely to overshoot into this permanently-negative regime.
  • High negative bias — a strongly negative bias term shifts the weighted sum toward negative territory before the weights even get a chance to compensate.

Solutions

  • Lower the learning rate — smaller steps are less likely to knock a neuron permanently into the dead zone.
  • Initialize bias slightly positive (e.g. 0.01) — gives the neuron a small head start on the positive side, rather than starting it right at the edge of dying.
  • Use a ReLU variant that is never fully flat on the negative side — the practical, most common fix, covered below.

ReLU variants split into two families: linear variants, which stay piecewise-linear (like ReLU itself) but replace the flat zero with a small non-zero slope, and non-linear variants, which curve smoothly on the negative side instead.

Linear variants

Leaky ReLU

$$ f(z) = \max(0.01z,\; z) $$

Instead of a hard zero for negative inputs, it lets a tiny slope (0.01) through. The gradient is now always either 1 or 0.01 — never exactly zero — so a neuron can never fully die; it can always eventually recover.

Parametric ReLU (PReLU)

Same shape as Leaky ReLU, \( f(z) = \max(\alpha z, z) \), except the negative-side slope \( \alpha \) is no longer a fixed 0.01 — it's a learnable parameter, updated by gradient descent along with everything else, letting the network decide the best slope for itself rather than guessing one in advance.

Non-linear variants

ELU (Exponential Linear Unit)

$$ f(z) = \begin{cases} z & z > 0 \\ \alpha(e^{z}-1) & z \le 0 \end{cases} $$

For negative inputs, ELU curves smoothly toward \( -\alpha \) instead of a straight line, and is always continuous (no sharp corner at all, unlike ReLU and Leaky ReLU which both bend abruptly at z=0). In practice it often outperforms plain ReLU, at the cost of being more expensive to compute (it involves an exponential).

SELU (Scaled ELU)

ELU multiplied by a carefully chosen scale constant, designed so that — under specific conditions on weight initialization (LeCun normal) and network architecture (plain feed-forward, no skip connections) — activations across layers automatically self-normalize toward zero mean and unit variance as they pass through the network, without needing a separate batch normalization layer.

Code — Using ReLU variants in Keras

from tensorflow.keras.layers import Dense, LeakyReLU, PReLU

model = Sequential([
    Dense(128, input_shape=(20,)),
    LeakyReLU(alpha=0.01),      # fixed small negative slope

    Dense(64),
    PReLU(),                    # learnable negative slope

    Dense(32, activation='elu'),   # ELU built into Keras directly
    Dense(1, activation='sigmoid')
])

Hard

5 questions on Activation Functions. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. Why does stacking layers with only linear activations fail to add any representational power?

Explanation: Algebraically, g₂(g₁(x)) = w₂(w₁x+b₁)+b₂ simplifies to a single (w,b) pair — no matter how many linear layers are stacked, the whole thing is mathematically identical to one linear regression.

2. Which pair correctly matches an ideal-activation-function property to why it matters?

Explanation: Saturating functions squeeze inputs into a narrow range and go nearly flat far from the origin, driving their derivative toward zero — the direct mechanism behind vanishing gradients. Non-saturating functions like ReLU avoid this in their active region.

3. Why is sigmoid still commonly used in the output layer of a binary classifier, despite being a poor hidden-layer choice?

Explanation: Sigmoid's [0,1] range maps cleanly onto a probability of the positive class. Since it's used only once, at the very end, its saturation and non-zero-centered weaknesses don't compound the way they would if it were used across every hidden layer of a deep network.

4. A network trained with a high learning rate and a strongly negative bias initialization has 70% of its ReLU neurons outputting exactly zero for every input. What's happening, and what's the standard practical fix?

Explanation: With more than 50% of neurons stuck at zero, this is a textbook dying ReLU scenario, and both listed causes (high LR, strongly negative bias) match. The standard fixes are exactly those listed: lower LR, small positive bias like 0.01, or a ReLU variant with a non-zero negative-side gradient.

5. What distinguishes ELU from Leaky ReLU and PReLU?

Explanation: Leaky ReLU and PReLU are piecewise-linear (a straight negative-side slope, sharply bending at z=0). ELU instead curves smoothly via an exponential term, making it continuous at z=0 with no sharp corner at all.
