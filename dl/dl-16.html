16. Sequence-to-Sequence & Encoder-Decoder

When input and output lengths differ · the Encoder-Decoder architecture · a full worked translation example · teacher forcing · the fixed-context-vector bottleneck

The last two pages fixed a real problem — plain RNNs forget, LSTM/GRU remember much better. But both of them share a quiet assumption: they read one sequence and either summarize it into a single label, or emit one output per input timestep. Machine translation, summarization, and chatbots break that assumption completely — the output isn't the same length as the input, and might not even be knowable in advance. This page covers the architecture invented specifically to handle that: the Encoder-Decoder, the foundation that Attention (next page) and eventually Transformers were built to improve on.

Seq2Seq

What Makes a Problem "Seq2Seq"

Recall the five RNN "shapes" from two pages ago. Four of them are comparatively easy: one-to-many, many-to-one, and many-to-many-with-matching-length all have a predictable relationship between input length and output length. The fifth — many-to-many with variable length — is genuinely hard, and it's common enough to deserve its own name: Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq).

A Seq2Seq problem is any task where both the input and the output are sequences, and their lengths don't have to match. Machine translation is the canonical example: "I love deep learning" (4 English words) might become "J'adore l'apprentissage profond" (a completely different word count in French) — there's no rule that says a 4-word input produces a 4-word output. The same shape shows up in text summarization (many sentences in, a few sentences out), question-answering (a question in, an answer of unpredictable length out), chatbots, and speech-to-text.

Why a single LSTM can't just do this. An LSTM handles variable-length input easily — it just loops through the sequence one timestep at a time, whether that's 5 words or 50, updating its hidden/cell state as it goes. But generating a variable-length output is a fundamentally different problem: the model doesn't know the output length in advance, each output word depends on words the model itself has already generated (not just on the input), and a plain LSTM has no built-in way to say "start generating now" or "stop, I'm done." Seq2Seq needs two separate roles — one network to read and compress the input, another to generate the output word by word — which is exactly why the architecture splits into an encoder and a decoder.

Seq2Seq

The Encoder-Decoder Architecture

The reference paper here is Sutskever, Vinyals & Le's "Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks" (2014), and the architecture it introduces is built from two separate LSTM (or GRU) networks with clearly divided jobs.

The Encoder — reads and compresses

Takes the input sequence one word at a time — e.g. "I am a student." Each word is fed into an LSTM cell at its own timestep; the LSTM updates its hidden state \(h\) and cell state \(c\) at every step, carrying forward everything it has understood so far, exactly as covered in the LSTM page. After the last input word is processed, the encoder's final hidden state and cell state together become the context vector — a fixed-size, compressed numerical summary of the entire input sentence's meaning. Think of it like reading a whole sentence, then closing your eyes and holding only the gist of it in your head — that gist is the context vector.

The Decoder — generates, one word at a time

The decoder's own LSTM doesn't start "blank." Its initial hidden state and cell state are set equal to the encoder's final hidden/cell state — decoder_initial_state = encoder_final_state. The decoder inherits the encoder's compressed understanding as its own starting memory; that's how the context vector actually gets used in this architecture — not fed in fresh at every step, just planted once as the decoder's starting point. A special <START> token is then fed in as the decoder's first input, telling it "begin generating now." At each timestep the decoder predicts the next word, that predicted (or, during training, the true) word is fed back in as the input for the following timestep, and this continues until the decoder outputs a special <END> token telling the system to stop.

Why <START> and <END> are necessary at all: without them the decoder has no way to know when to begin (there's no "real" previous word to feed in at the very first decoder timestep, so <START> is an artificial first input, essentially a "go" signal) or when to stop (output length is variable and unknown ahead of time, so without <END> the model would keep generating indefinitely, or you'd need to hardcode a crude maximum length).

Illustrative aid — generated for this site to visualize the architecture.

Read left to right: the encoder's three LSTM cells each take one input word (arriving from below) plus the hidden/cell state handed off from the previous cell, and after the last word ("fine"), the final \((h_3, c_3)\) becomes the context vector — shown as the bridge box in the middle. That context vector becomes the starting hidden/cell state for the decoder's first cell, which then runs the same left-to-right chain, except now each cell also produces a predicted word (via a softmax layer, shown above each decoder cell) at every step.

Seq2Seq

Embeddings In, Context Vector Out

Neither the encoder nor the decoder actually sees raw words — every word is first converted into a dense numerical embedding vector by an embedding layer that sits directly below the LSTM cells, on both the encoder and decoder side. This is a big improvement over one-hot encoding, where each word is a huge, mostly-zero vector (as large as the entire vocabulary) that carries no notion of meaning — under one-hot encoding, "cat" and "dog" are just as "different" from each other as "cat" and "car." Embeddings instead represent each word as a small, dense vector (e.g. 100 or 300 numbers, no forced zeros), learned so that semantically similar words end up close together in that vector space — for example, "I", "am", and "fine" might become [0.21, -0.05, 0.88, ...], [0.11, 0.42, -0.09, ...], and [0.67, -0.31, 0.15, ...] respectively. These embeddings can either be pretrained (Word2Vec, GloVe — trained separately on huge text corpora, useful when your own training data is limited) or trained from scratch as part of the Seq2Seq network end-to-end, tailored to the specific task.

It's easy to confuse an embedding with the context vector, since both are "just vectors of numbers" — but they represent fundamentally different things:

Embedding Context Vector
What it representsA single word or token's meaningThe meaning of the entire input sequence
When it's createdBefore/as input enters the network (a lookup)After the encoder has processed the whole sequence
Fixed or contextualStatic per word (in classic embeddings like Word2Vec)Built dynamically depending on everything seen so far
AnalogyA dictionary definition of one wordA one-paragraph summary of an entire book

In short: embeddings go in, one per word; the context vector comes out, once, after the encoder finishes reading the whole sentence.

Seq2Seq

Worked Example — Training on "I am fine" → "Je vais bien"

Walking through one full training example end to end makes every piece above concrete. The toy task: translate the English sentence "I am fine" into French, "Je vais bien."

Step 1 — add boundary tokens to the target. The target sequence is first wrapped in the special tokens: <START> Je vais bien <END>. This is what the decoder is trained to predict, one token at a time.

Step 2 — encoder forward pass, word by word

TimestepInput wordWhat happens
t1"I"embedding("I") → LSTM cell → updates h₁, c₁
t2"am"embedding("am") → LSTM cell (using h₁, c₁) → updates h₂, c₂
t3"fine"embedding("fine") → LSTM cell (using h₂, c₂) → updates h₃, c₃

After t3, \((h_3, c_3)\) is the context vector — the encoder has now "read and understood" the whole sentence.

Step 3 — decoder forward pass, with teacher forcing

The decoder's LSTM starts with \((h_3, c_3)\) as its own initial state.

TimestepDecoder inputSoftmax predictsActual target
d1<START>"Je" (hopefully)"Je"
d2"Je" (true word, not the model's own guess)"vais""vais"
d3"vais" (true word)"bien""bien"
d4"bien" (true word)<END><END>
The softmax output layer, at every decoder timestep: the LSTM cell's hidden state is passed through a Dense layer whose output size equals the entire vocabulary (e.g. 50,000 nodes for a 50,000-word vocabulary), then a softmax activation converts those raw scores into a probability distribution over every possible word — "here's how likely each word in the vocabulary is to be the correct next word." The highest-probability word is picked as the prediction (this is greedy decoding; more advanced systems use beam search instead). The output layer needs one slot per vocabulary word because the decoder isn't choosing from some small fixed set — it has to be able to produce any word in the target language at any step.
Loss — categorical cross-entropy. At every decoder timestep the model is doing multi-class classification — picking one word out of the entire vocabulary — so the loss is categorical cross-entropy, not MSE (MSE would treat word indices as if they were continuous numbers, which makes no sense for categorical labels). Since the true label is one-hot, the loss for one timestep simplifies to \( -\log(\text{predicted probability of the correct word}) \): confident-and-correct predictions produce a loss near 0, confident-and-wrong predictions are punished heavily. The total loss for the training example is the sum (or average) across all decoder timesteps, then backpropagation computes the gradient of that loss with respect to every weight (encoder LSTM, decoder LSTM, embeddings, softmax), and an optimizer like Adam uses those gradients to update every weight slightly — repeated across thousands or millions of sentence pairs until the model generalizes well.
Seq2Seq

Teacher Forcing — Training on the Ground Truth, Not the Guess

Look again at the worked example's decoder table above: at every step, the input fed to the next timestep is the true previous word ("Je," "vais," "bien"), not whatever the decoder actually predicted. This deliberate choice is called teacher forcing, and it exists to solve a real training problem.

During training, the "natural" thing to feed into the decoder at each step would be whatever it just predicted — that's exactly what happens later at inference time, when there's no ground truth available to fall back on. But early in training the model is bad at this task. If it predicts garbage at timestep 2 and that garbage gets fed into timestep 3, the error compounds: the model spends the rest of the sequence trying to recover from a bad start instead of actually learning the next-word patterns it's supposed to be learning. Teacher forcing's fix is simple: during training, regardless of what the decoder actually predicted, always feed the ground-truth word as the input to the next timestep.

Concretely: suppose at d2 the true word is "vais," but the model incorrectly predicts "suis." With teacher forcing, "vais" (the correct word) is still what gets fed into d3 — not the model's wrong guess "suis." Every timestep is trained on a clean signal this way, so learning is faster and more stable, and the loss at each step reflects that step's own prediction quality rather than being polluted by an earlier mistake compounding forward.

The trade-off: exposure bias. Teacher forcing creates a mismatch between training and inference. During training, the decoder always sees correct previous words; at inference, it only ever has its own (possibly wrong) previous predictions to condition on. This means the model can behave noticeably worse at inference than it appeared to during training, especially once an early error occurs and starts compounding — a gap known as exposure bias. Refinements like scheduled sampling (gradually mixing in the model's own predictions during training, rather than using pure ground truth throughout) exist specifically to narrow this gap, though the original Sutskever et al. architecture uses full teacher forcing throughout.

Seq2Seq

Three Practical Improvements From the Original Paper

Deep (stacked) LSTMs

Instead of one row of LSTM cells, stack several rows on top of each other (the original paper used 4 layers in both encoder and decoder). Each layer's output at a given timestep feeds both forward in time (to the next timestep, same layer) and upward (into the next layer at the same timestep) — similar in spirit to how CNNs build from edges to shapes to objects, lower LSTM layers tend to pick up local, word-level patterns while higher layers build more abstract, sentence-level meaning. More layers means more capacity to capture subtle patterns, at the cost of more computation and a higher risk of overfitting on small datasets. In a stacked encoder-decoder, the encoder-to-decoder handoff happens per layer — a 3-layer stack hands off 3 separate \((h,c)\) pairs, one per layer.

Reversing the encoder input

A surprising trick from the original paper: feed the input sentence in reverse order ("student a am I" instead of "I am a student"). Since an LSTM's hidden state is most strongly influenced by whatever it processed most recently, reversing the input places the first word of the source sentence right next to the start of decoding, shortening the effective distance information has to travel between corresponding words — introducing many short-range dependencies even in an overall long sentence, which made optimization noticeably easier in practice. It's a practical trick rather than a universal law (it helps most when sentence structure roughly aligns front-to-front across the two languages), and attention — up next — makes it largely unnecessary, since attention lets the decoder access any encoder timestep directly rather than depending on recency.

Seq2Seq

The Encoder-Decoder Bottleneck

This architecture works well for short sentences. But look again at what actually gets passed from encoder to decoder: one fixed-size vector, \((h_3, c_3)\) — regardless of whether the input sentence had 3 words or 30. Every bit of meaning in the entire input has to be squeezed through that single, fixed-capacity channel.

Why this is a genuine information bottleneck. For long sequences, compressing the entire meaning into one fixed-size context vector causes real information loss — early words in a long sentence get diluted or effectively forgotten by the time the encoder finishes reading the rest. The decoder, meanwhile, has no way to "look back" at specific parts of the input once decoding starts; it's stuck reusing that same single static summary at every output timestep, even though different output words logically depend on different parts of the input sentence. A single vector simply cannot scale to represent long, detailed sequences — it's a hard capacity limit baked into the architecture itself, not something more training or a bigger LSTM alone can fix.

This puts unfair pressure on both halves of the network at once: the encoder has to anticipate and pack in everything the decoder might ever need, all before the decoder has even started generating anything, and the decoder is then stuck reusing that same static summary at every single output step. A fixed-size vector is an information bottleneck — and it's exactly this bottleneck that the next page's topic, Attention, was invented to remove: instead of relying on one final context vector, the decoder gets access to all of the encoder's hidden states, and dynamically decides — freshly, at every single decoding step — which parts of the input actually matter right now.

Test Your Understanding

Hard

5 questions on Seq2Seq & the Encoder-Decoder. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. Why can't a single plain LSTM be used directly for a task like machine translation?

Explanation: Variable-length input is the easy part — an LSTM just loops through it. Generating a variable-length output is fundamentally different: the model must decide length, condition on its own generated tokens, and know when to stop — exactly why the encoder/decoder split exists.

2. What exactly is passed from the encoder to the decoder in the vanilla (Sutskever et al.) architecture?

Explanation: decoder_initial_state = encoder_final_state. Only the final (h, c) — the context vector — is handed off; every earlier encoder hidden state is discarded. That's precisely the design attention later fixes.

3. During training with teacher forcing, the decoder predicts "suis" at timestep d2 but the true word is "vais." What is fed into the decoder as input at timestep d3?

Explanation: That's the definition of teacher forcing — always feed the true previous word during training, so errors don't compound across timesteps. The cost is exposure bias, since inference has no ground truth to fall back on.

4. Why is categorical cross-entropy the correct loss function for training the decoder, rather than MSE?

Explanation: Every decoder timestep picks one word out of the whole vocabulary via softmax — a multi-class classification problem, which categorical cross-entropy is designed for. MSE doesn't make sense over categorical, one-hot targets.

5. What is the fundamental limitation of the vanilla (no-attention) Encoder-Decoder architecture that motivates Attention?

Explanation: One fixed-size vector must represent an entire input sequence regardless of its length — for long sentences this causes real information loss, and the decoder has no way to revisit specific input words. Attention removes exactly this bottleneck by giving the decoder access to every encoder hidden state.
