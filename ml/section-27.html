27. K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)

"Judged by the company you keep" · distance metrics · choosing K · the curse of dimensionality strikes again

Extended notes — this topic isn't covered in the CampusX playlist notes this site is based on. Written from scratch to continue the curriculum, in the same style as the rest of the site.

Every model so far — Linear/Logistic Regression, Ridge/Lasso — works by learning parameters during training: a fixed set of weights that summarize the whole dataset, after which the training data itself can be thrown away. KNN is a completely different philosophy: it doesn't learn anything at training time. It just remembers the data, and does all its "thinking" at prediction time by looking at what's nearby.

Classification & Regression

What is KNN? Full Overview

The whole algorithm is one sentence: to predict a new point, find the K training points closest to it, and let them vote (or average). That's it — no cost function, no gradient descent, no coefficients to learn.

Analogy — you're judged by the company you keep

Imagine you move to a new neighborhood and someone asks "is this person trustworthy?" One reasonable heuristic: look at their five closest friends. If four of the five are known to be trustworthy, guess that this new person probably is too. You're not analyzing the person directly — you're borrowing the label of whoever is most similar to (closest to) them. That's exactly what KNN does with data points instead of people, and "closeness" measured by a distance formula instead of friendship.

Because it stores the entire training set and defers all computation to prediction time, KNN is called a lazy learner (as opposed to an eager learner like logistic regression, which does all its work upfront during .fit() and predicts in constant time afterward). It's also called instance-based learning — the "model" isn't a formula, it's literally just the stored training instances.

Task type — both. KNN is one of the few algorithms on this site that natively does Classification (predict a category) and Regression (predict a number) with the exact same neighbor-finding step — only the last step (vote vs. average) differs.
Classification & Regression

How It Works

For a new point \(x\): compute the distance from \(x\) to every point in the training set, sort by distance, keep the K closest, then combine their labels.

Distance metrics

MetricFormulaIntuition
Euclidean (L2)\( \sqrt{\sum_i (x_i-y_i)^2} \)Straight-line ("as the crow flies") distance — the default, most common choice
Manhattan (L1)\( \sum_i |x_i-y_i| \)"City block" distance — sum of moves along each axis, like walking a rectangular street grid instead of cutting diagonally
Minkowski\( \left(\sum_i |x_i-y_i|^p\right)^{1/p} \)The general form — p=2 gives Euclidean, p=1 gives Manhattan; sklearn's default metric, tunable via its p parameter

Combining the K neighbors' labels

Classification → majority vote. Look at the K neighbors' class labels, predict whichever class appears most often. (Ties are typically broken by falling back to the single nearest neighbor, or by using an odd K to avoid ties in binary problems.)
Regression → averaging. Take the mean (sometimes distance-weighted) of the K neighbors' target values. If your 5 nearest houses sold for $300k, $310k, $295k, $320k, $305k, predict roughly $306k.

Code — KNN for both tasks

from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier, KNeighborsRegressor
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

# --- Classification ---
clf = make_pipeline(
    StandardScaler(),                      # essential — see "when to avoid" below
    KNeighborsClassifier(
        n_neighbors=5,
        metric='minkowski', p=2,           # p=2 -> Euclidean distance
        weights='distance'                 # closer neighbors count more than farther ones
    )
)
clf.fit(X_train, y_train)
y_pred = clf.predict(X_test)

# --- Regression --- (identical shape, different output type)
reg = make_pipeline(
    StandardScaler(),
    KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=5, weights='distance')
)
reg.fit(X_train, y_train_continuous)
y_pred_continuous = reg.predict(X_test)
Note. weights='distance' gives closer neighbors more say than farther ones (typically weighted by 1/distance) instead of every one of the K neighbors getting an equal vote (weights='uniform', the default) — often a small but free accuracy improvement.
Classification & Regression

The Effect of K — Bias-Variance in Action

K is KNN's single most important hyperparameter, and it maps directly onto Section 21's Bias-Variance Tradeoff — arguably more transparently than any other model on this site.

Small K (e.g. K=1)

The prediction is decided by a single nearest neighbor. The decision boundary becomes extremely jagged, wrapping tightly around individual points — including mislabeled or noisy ones. This is overfitting: low bias (it can represent very complex boundaries) but high variance — a different training sample (or one flipped label) can swing predictions dramatically.

Large K (e.g. K=N)

With K equal to the whole training set, every prediction is just "the overall majority class" (or the global mean, for regression) — completely ignoring where the query point actually sits. This is underfitting: the boundary becomes smooth to the point of uselessness — high bias, low variance.

The right K sits in between, and — just like polynomial degree in Section 20 or \(\lambda\) in Section 22 — is found empirically: sweep a range of K values, measure validation error (or run cross-validation) for each, and pick the K at the bottom of the U-shaped error curve.

Code — sweeping K with cross-validation

from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score
import numpy as np

k_values = range(1, 31)
mean_scores = []

for k in k_values:
    model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k))
    scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=5, scoring='accuracy')
    mean_scores.append(scores.mean())

best_k = k_values[np.argmax(mean_scores)]
print(f"best K = {best_k}, CV accuracy = {max(mean_scores):.3f}")
# plotting mean_scores against k_values shows the classic bias-variance "U" —
# low accuracy at very small k (noisy), rising, then falling again at large k (oversmoothed)
Concept

Why the Curse of Dimensionality Hits KNN Especially Hard

Section 17 introduced the curse of dimensionality: as the number of features grows, the volume of the feature space explodes, and data points become sparse — everything ends up far from everything else. No algorithm on this site depends more directly on the idea of "distance = meaningful similarity" than KNN, which makes it the textbook victim of this effect.

In high dimensions, a striking thing happens to distances: the ratio between the distance to the nearest point and the distance to the farthest point tends toward 1. In plain English — everyone starts looking roughly equally far away. If your "5 nearest neighbors" aren't meaningfully closer to the query point than the other 995 points in a 1,000-point dataset, then "nearest" has stopped carrying useful information, and KNN's core assumption (nearby points are similar) breaks down.

Practical takeaway. With very high-dimensional data (hundreds of features), consider dimensionality reduction (Section 18's PCA) before applying KNN, or favor an algorithm that doesn't rely on raw distance at all.
Practical

When to Use KNN — and When to Avoid It

Good fit when...

  • The dataset is small to medium sized
  • The number of features is modest (curse of dimensionality kept at bay)
  • You want a fast-to-build baseline with almost no tuning
  • The true decision boundary is irregular/non-linear and you'd rather not hand-engineer polynomial features

Avoid / be careful when...

  • Large datasets — prediction requires scanning (in the naive case) every training point, making inference slow at large N; there is no "trained model," only stored data
  • Many features — curse of dimensionality erodes the meaning of "nearest"
  • Unscaled features — KNN is purely distance-based, so a feature ranging 0–100,000 (like income) will completely dominate a feature ranging 0–1 (like a normalized score) unless you scale first; feature scaling isn't optional here, it's required
  • You need to explain why a prediction was made in terms of learned rules or coefficients — KNN has none, only "these were the closest points"
Rule of thumb: KNN is cheap to train (there is no training) but expensive to predict — the exact opposite trade-off of most parametric models like logistic regression, which are expensive to train but essentially free to predict afterward.

Test Your Understanding

Hard

5 questions on KNN. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. Why is KNN called a "lazy learner"?

Explanation: "Lazy" refers to the training/prediction split: eager learners (logistic regression, etc.) do all their work at fit-time and predict instantly; KNN's fit() just stores data, deferring (being "lazy" about) all real computation until you actually ask for a prediction.

2. A KNN classifier with K=1 gets 100% training accuracy but poor test accuracy. In bias-variance terms, what's happening?

Explanation: K=1 always predicts using a single nearest neighbor, which means training accuracy is always 100% (every training point is its own nearest neighbor) — a classic overfitting signature. This is a low-bias, high-variance model, exactly Section 21's overfitting corner.

3. Why must features typically be scaled (e.g. with StandardScaler) before fitting KNN?

Explanation: Euclidean/Manhattan distance is computed directly from raw feature values. A feature measured in the tens of thousands (income) will swamp a feature measured on a 0-1 scale in the distance formula, effectively making the model ignore the smaller-scale feature entirely — regardless of its true predictive value.

4. In a 500-dimensional feature space, you notice that the distance from a query point to its "nearest" neighbor is almost the same as the distance to its "farthest" neighbor. What does this signal?

Explanation: This near-nearest/farthest ratio collapsing toward 1 is the textbook symptom of the curse of dimensionality — a direct threat to KNN, whose entire premise is that "close" points are meaningfully more similar than "far" points.

5. Compared to logistic regression, what is KNN's training-vs-prediction time trade-off?

Explanation: KNN defers all work to prediction time (must scan/compare against stored training points), while logistic regression does all its work during fit() via iterative optimization and then predicts with one cheap dot product — an inverted cost profile.
