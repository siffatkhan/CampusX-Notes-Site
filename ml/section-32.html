32. Random Forest

Bagging of trees + random feature subsampling · why it works (bias-variance) · hyperparameters & tuning · OOB score · feature importance

Extended notes: This topic isn't covered in the CampusX playlist notes this site is based on. It's written from scratch to continue the curriculum, in the same style as the rest of the site.
Classification & Regression

Intro & Intuition

Random Forest is the most popular ensemble algorithm in classical ML. It's hard to misuse, needs almost no feature scaling or preprocessing, and it's often a strong baseline right out of the box. It's built from two ideas you already know, combined.

Source of randomness #1: Bootstrap rows

Like Bagging (previous page), each tree trains on its own bootstrap sample: N rows drawn with replacement from the training set. Different trees see different, overlapping subsets of the rows.

Source of randomness #2: Random feature subsets

New in Random Forest: at every split in every tree, the algorithm considers only a random subset of the features as candidates for splitting, not all of them. It draws a different random subset at every split, in every tree.

Why both matter: bootstrap rows and random subset of columns

Row-bootstrapping alone (plain bagging) already makes trees somewhat different from each other. But say one feature is extremely predictive. In a churn dataset, "days since last login" might be far more informative than everything else combined. Even with different bootstrapped rows, every bagged tree tends to pick that same feature for its first (root) split, because it's the best split available on almost any resample. That makes the trees more similar, more correlated than you'd like. As you'll see in the next section, this limits how much variance the ensemble can cancel out.

By hiding a random subset of columns at each split too, Random Forest sometimes forces a tree to split on the second-best or third-best feature instead. The strongest feature simply wasn't offered as an option at that node. This decorrelates the trees: different trees build around different features and find different patterns in the data. Each tree is sometimes slightly worse on its own, since it didn't always get to use the best feature, but the ensemble as a whole becomes better. Averaging over more diverse, less correlated trees removes more variance.

In one sentence: Random Forest is Bagging of Decision Trees, plus random feature subsampling at every split for extra diversity.

Concept

How Random Forest Performs So Well: Bias-Variance Tradeoff in RF

This is the most important part of understanding why Random Forest works, so it's worth slowing down for.

Individual trees: low bias, high variance

A single, fully grown (unpruned) Decision Tree keeps splitting until its leaves are pure or tiny. That flexibility lets it fit almost any pattern in its training data, so it has low bias. But the same flexibility lets it fit noise too, so it has high variance. Train the same unpruned tree on two different bootstrap samples from the same dataset, and you get two visibly different trees that disagree on plenty of predictions.

Averaging many decorrelated trees crushes variance, barely raises bias

Here's the key statistical fact underlying the whole algorithm. Suppose you average \(n\) trees, each with variance \(\sigma^2\), and the average pairwise correlation between any two trees' predictions is \(\rho\). The variance of the averaged prediction is:

$$ \text{Var}(\text{average}) = \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{n}\,\sigma^2 $$

Notice two things. The second term shrinks toward 0 as \(n\) (the number of trees) grows. This is the averaging effect you already saw with bagging. But the first term, \(\rho\sigma^2\), never goes away, no matter how many trees you add. It's a floor set entirely by how correlated the trees are with each other. This is exactly why Random Forest's random feature subsampling matters so much: it directly lowers \(\rho\), which lowers the irreducible floor. That means adding more trees buys you more real variance reduction than it would in plain bagging, where \(\rho\) tends to be higher because every tree keeps reaching for the same dominant features.

Each individual tree is still deep and flexible, so the bias of the average is essentially the bias of one tree. Averaging predictions doesn't systematically push them in any particular wrong direction, it cancels noise. The net effect: you keep the near-zero bias of deep trees while pushing variance down. That combination, low bias and low variance, is what a single deep tree never gives you on its own.

Bagging vs Random Forest

Random Forest is Bagging applied specifically to Decision Trees, with one addition. The key difference: Random Forest adds feature randomness on top of bagging's row randomness. That extra decorrelation is why Random Forest usually beats plain bagged trees, given the same number of trees.

AspectBagging (generic)Random Forest
Base learnerAny model (often trees, but not required)Specifically Decision Trees
Row samplingBootstrap (with replacement)Bootstrap (with replacement)
Feature sampling per splitNone — every split considers all featuresRandom subset of features considered at each split
Tree-to-tree correlation ρHigherLower (deliberately decorrelated)
sklearn classBaggingClassifier / BaggingRegressor (with estimator=DecisionTree...)RandomForestClassifier / RandomForestRegressor

You could, in principle, reconstruct something close to Random Forest by hand using BaggingClassifier(estimator=DecisionTreeClassifier(max_features='sqrt')). But sklearn's dedicated RandomForestClassifier/RandomForestRegressor implement this more efficiently, and they expose extra conveniences directly, like OOB scoring and impurity-based feature importance, both covered below.

Random Forest Hyperparameters

HyperparameterWhat it controlsPractical guidance
n_estimatorsNumber of trees in the forestMore is almost always better for accuracy. RF doesn't overfit as you add trees, it just plateaus and costs more compute and memory. 100 to 500 is a common practical range. Check where the validation curve flattens.
max_depthMaximum depth of each individual treeDefault (None) grows trees fully. This is usually fine for RF since averaging controls variance. Limiting depth trades some variance-fighting power for faster training and inference, and it helps on noisy data.
max_featuresSize of the random feature subset considered at each split, the second source of randomnessThis is the most important RF-specific setting. Common defaults: sqrt(n_features) for classification, n_features (or 1/3) for regression. Smaller values mean more decorrelation, lower ρ, and potentially lower ensemble variance, but each split is weaker, with slightly higher per-tree bias. Tune it. It interacts directly with the bias-variance math above.
min_samples_leafMinimum number of samples required in a leaf nodeActs as regularization on each tree. Raising it, for example from 1 to 5-20, keeps leaves from fitting to single noisy points. It trades a bit of per-tree bias for less overfitting, useful on smaller or noisier datasets.
max_samplesFraction/count of rows drawn per bootstrap sample (only used when bootstrap=True)Lower than 1.0 increases per-tree diversity (more decorrelation, similar to max_features) at the cost of each tree seeing less data. It also directly affects the OOB fraction, see below.

Quick Code — Fitting a Random Forest

from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, random_state=1)

rf = RandomForestClassifier(
    n_estimators=300,
    max_depth=None,
    max_features='sqrt',   # random feature subset size per split
    min_samples_leaf=2,
    bootstrap=True,
    n_jobs=-1,
    random_state=1
)
rf.fit(X_train, y_train)
print("Test accuracy:", rf.score(X_test, y_test))

Hyperparameter Tuning with GridSearchCV & RandomizedSearchCV

With 5 interacting hyperparameters (plus more you haven't seen here, like criterion or max_leaf_nodes), guessing values by hand doesn't scale. sklearn gives you two systematic search tools.

GridSearchCV: Exhaustive Search

It tries every combination of the hyperparameter values you list, cross-validating each one. It's guaranteed to find the best combination within your grid, but the number of combinations, and the runtime with it, grows multiplicatively with every extra parameter or value you add.

from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier

param_grid = {
    'n_estimators': [100, 300, 500],
    'max_depth': [None, 10, 20],
    'max_features': ['sqrt', 'log2', 0.5],
    'min_samples_leaf': [1, 2, 5]
}
# 3 * 3 * 3 * 3 = 81 combinations, each fit with cv=5 -> 405 model fits total

grid = GridSearchCV(
    estimator=RandomForestClassifier(random_state=1, n_jobs=-1),
    param_grid=param_grid,
    cv=5,
    scoring='accuracy',
    n_jobs=-1,
    verbose=1
)
grid.fit(X_train, y_train)

print("Best params:", grid.best_params_)
print("Best CV score:", grid.best_score_)
best_rf = grid.best_estimator_

RandomizedSearchCV: Sampled Search

Instead of trying every combination, it randomly samples a fixed number (n_iter) of combinations from the parameter space, including continuous distributions, not only fixed lists. It doesn't guarantee the single best combination, but in practice it finds a good one far faster than an exhaustive grid.

from sklearn.model_selection import RandomizedSearchCV
from scipy.stats import randint, uniform

param_dist = {
    'n_estimators': randint(100, 800),
    'max_depth': [None, 10, 15, 20, 30],
    'max_features': uniform(0.2, 0.8),   # continuous range [0.2, 1.0]
    'min_samples_leaf': randint(1, 10)
}

random_search = RandomizedSearchCV(
    estimator=RandomForestClassifier(random_state=1, n_jobs=-1),
    param_distributions=param_dist,
    n_iter=50,          # only 50 random combinations tried, not all of them
    cv=5,
    scoring='accuracy',
    n_jobs=-1,
    random_state=1,
    verbose=1
)
random_search.fit(X_train, y_train)
print("Best params:", random_search.best_params_)

When to prefer RandomizedSearchCV

  • Large search spaces — with many hyperparameters or wide ranges, a full grid becomes computationally infeasible (combinations explode), while random search's cost is controlled directly by n_iter, independent of how many parameters you're searching.
  • Limited compute budget — you can cap total runtime up front by choosing n_iter, then stop; grid search either finishes the whole grid or you cancel it and lose the systematic guarantee.
  • Research has also shown that random search tends to explore parameter space more efficiently than grid search when only a few hyperparameters actually matter much for performance — grid search "wastes" many trials varying unimportant parameters at fixed values of the important ones.
  • Use GridSearchCV when the space is small and you want an exhaustive, reproducible guarantee; use RandomizedSearchCV (often followed by a small GridSearchCV around the best region found) for everything larger.
Concept

OOB Score — Out-of-Bag Evaluation

Bootstrap sampling has a neat side effect that Random Forest can exploit for free validation. Because each tree's bootstrap sample is drawn with replacement, some rows get picked multiple times — and, correspondingly, some rows don't get picked into that particular tree's sample at all. Those unpicked rows are that tree's out-of-bag (OOB) samples.

Where does the ~63% / ~37% split come from?

For a dataset of N rows, the probability that any one specific row is not chosen in a single random draw is \((1 - 1/N)\). Since a bootstrap sample makes N independent draws (with replacement), the probability that a given row is never picked across all N draws is \((1 - 1/N)^N\). As N gets large, this converges to:

$$ \lim_{N\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)^N = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.368 $$

So roughly 36.8% of the rows are left out of any given tree's bootstrap sample (its OOB set), and the remaining ~63.2% are used to train it — this holds for any reasonably large N, which is why you'll see it stated as the "~63/37 rule."

Why this gives you a free validation estimate

Every row in your training set is "out-of-bag" for some subset of the trees (roughly 37% of them, on average) — trees that never saw that row during training. So for each training row, you can collect predictions only from the trees that didn't train on it, aggregate those (vote or average, same as usual), and compare against the true label. Do this for every row and average the result — that's the OOB score: an estimate of generalization performance computed entirely from training data, without carving out a separate holdout/validation set at all.

This is especially valuable with smaller datasets, where you don't want to sacrifice training rows to a validation split — OOB scoring effectively gives you validation-like performance "for free" as a side effect of how the ensemble was already trained. It's not a full substitute for a proper held-out test set (especially for final reporting), but it's an excellent, nearly-zero-cost sanity check during model development.

Code — OOB Score

rf = RandomForestClassifier(
    n_estimators=300,
    oob_score=True,   # ask sklearn to compute the OOB estimate while fitting
    bootstrap=True,    # OOB requires bootstrap sampling to be enabled
    n_jobs=-1,
    random_state=1
)
rf.fit(X_train, y_train)   # no separate validation split needed

print("OOB score:", rf.oob_score_)          # generalization estimate from training data alone
print("Test score:", rf.score(X_test, y_test))  # for comparison, on a true held-out set
Concept

Feature Importance using Random Forest & Decision Trees

Trees split by choosing, at each node, the feature and threshold that most reduces impurity (Gini/entropy for classification, variance/MSE for regression — see the Decision Trees page). That gives every split a natural "how much did this feature help here?" score, which Random Forest turns into a full feature-ranking tool for free.

How impurity-based (Gini) importance is computed

  1. For every split in every tree, compute the impurity decrease that split achieved, weighted by the fraction of samples reaching that node.
  2. Attribute that decrease to whichever feature was used for the split.
  3. Sum a feature's attributed decreases across all splits in one tree, then average across all trees in the forest.
  4. Normalize so all features' importances sum to 1.

The result — rf.feature_importances_ in sklearn — is a single number per feature: roughly, "how much impurity reduction, on average across the whole forest, is this feature responsible for."

Code — Reading Off Feature Importances

import pandas as pd

rf.fit(X_train, y_train)

importances = pd.Series(rf.feature_importances_, index=X_train.columns)
importances.sort_values(ascending=False).plot(kind='bar')

print(importances.sort_values(ascending=False).head(10))

Known bias: high-cardinality features

Impurity-based importance is systematically biased toward features with many possible split points — high-cardinality categorical features (like a "user ID" or "zip code" column) or continuous numeric features. These features get more chances to be picked as a locally-optimal split purely because they offer more candidate thresholds to try, even if they carry little real predictive signal — sometimes even a completely random, meaningless high-cardinality column ends up ranked as "important" this way. Low-cardinality features (like a binary flag) can be systematically underrated for the opposite reason.

The more robust alternative: Permutation Importance

Instead of reading importance off the tree structure, permutation importance measures it empirically: take a trained model and a validation set, shuffle (permute) the values of one feature column (breaking its relationship with the target while keeping everything else intact), and measure how much the model's performance drops. A big drop means the model relied heavily on that feature; almost no drop means the feature wasn't doing much. Repeat per feature. Because it's measured on held-out data and doesn't care how many split points a feature offers, it isn't biased toward high-cardinality features the way impurity-based importance is — at the cost of being slower to compute (it requires re-scoring the model many times).

from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance

result = permutation_importance(rf, X_test, y_test, n_repeats=10, random_state=1, n_jobs=-1)
perm_importances = pd.Series(result.importances_mean, index=X_test.columns)
print(perm_importances.sort_values(ascending=False).head(10))

Hard

5 questions on Random Forest. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. In the formula Var(average) = ρσ² + (1−ρ)σ²/n, what happens to ensemble variance as n → ∞ with ρ fixed at 0.3?

Explanation: As n→∞, the (1-ρ)σ²/n term vanishes, leaving only ρσ² = 0.3σ² as an irreducible floor. This is exactly why decorrelating trees (lowering ρ via max_features) matters more, past a point, than simply adding more trees.

2. Why does setting max_features to a smaller value (e.g. sqrt(n_features) instead of all features) typically help a random forest, despite making each individual split "weaker"?

Explanation: Hiding features at each split forces trees away from always picking the single dominant feature, decorrelating them (lower ρ). Per the bias-variance breakdown, that lowers the ensemble's irreducible variance floor — usually a bigger win than the small per-tree bias increase from occasionally missing the globally-best feature.

3. Roughly what fraction of the original training rows are, on average, out-of-bag for any single tree in a random forest trained with the default bootstrap settings (max_samples = 1.0 · N)?

Explanation: The probability a specific row is never drawn across N with-replacement draws is (1-1/N)^N → 1/e ≈ 0.368 as N grows — so about 37% of rows are OOB for a given tree, and the complementary ~63% are in-bag (used for training that tree).

4. Your dataset has a "customer_id" column (nearly unique per row) that you accidentally left in as a feature. Impurity-based feature_importances_ ranks it as the 2nd most important feature, even though it's pure noise with respect to the target. What best explains this?

Explanation: This is the classic, well-documented failure mode of Gini/impurity-based feature importance: a feature with many candidate split thresholds (like a near-unique ID column) has more opportunities to produce a split that happens to reduce impurity on the training data purely by chance, so it can rank spuriously high. Permutation importance, computed on held-out data, is much more robust to this.

5. You need to search 6 hyperparameters, several with wide continuous ranges, and have a strict compute budget of 2 hours. Which tuning approach fits best, and why?

Explanation: With 6 parameters (several continuous) and a hard time budget, an exhaustive grid explodes combinatorially and can't be capped without shrinking the grid itself. RandomizedSearchCV lets you fix n_iter to whatever fits your budget and samples directly from continuous distributions (e.g. via scipy.stats), making it the practical choice here. (cv=1 is also invalid — cross-validation requires at least 2 folds.)
