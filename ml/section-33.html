33. AdaBoost

Sequential boosting · weighted samples · alpha ("say") weighting · sklearn AdaBoostClassifier/Regressor · GridSearchCV

Extended notes — this topic isn't covered in the CampusX playlist notes this site is based on. Written from scratch to continue the curriculum, in the same style as the rest of the site.
Concept

Geometric Intuition — Sequential Correction, Not Parallel Independence

Bagging and Random Forest (previous two pages) are both parallel ensembles: every tree is trained independently, on its own bootstrap sample, with zero knowledge of what any other tree is doing. You could train all of them at the same time on different machines and it wouldn't change the result at all. AdaBoost — short for Adaptive Boosting — throws that independence away on purpose. It's the first member of the boosting family, where models are built one at a time, in strict sequence, and each new model is deliberately trained to clean up the specific mistakes of the one before it.

Bagging / Random Forest — parallel

  • All base models trained independently, at the same time in principle
  • No model ever "sees" another model's errors
  • Final prediction: simple majority vote / average — every model counts equally
  • Goal: reduce variance by averaging out noise

AdaBoost / Boosting — sequential

  • Base models trained one after another, in strict order
  • Each new model is trained specifically to focus on the previous model's mistakes
  • Final prediction: weighted vote — more accurate learners get more "say"
  • Goal: reduce bias by chaining together many weak learners into one strong one

What's a "weak learner," and why a decision stump?

AdaBoost's base models are deliberately kept weak — typically a decision stump: a decision tree with just one split (depth 1). A single stump is barely better than random guessing on its own — that's the point. AdaBoost's trick is showing that you can chain together a sequence of these barely-better-than-random models and, by having each one specialize in what the previous ones got wrong and then combining them with smart weighting, end up with an ensemble that's a very strong learner overall.

The mechanism for "focusing on previous mistakes" is sample reweighting. Every training point carries a weight. Points the current ensemble gets wrong have their weight increased before the next stump is trained, so the next stump's own error metric is dominated by exactly those hard points — it's forced to pay attention to them or it will score badly itself.

In one sentence: AdaBoost = a sequence of weak learners, each one retrained on a reweighted version of the data that emphasizes the previous learner's mistakes, combined via a weighted vote where more accurate learners get more say. The very next page puts this side-by-side against bagging in full detail.

Classification

Step-by-Step: How AdaBoost Trains

  1. Initialize equal sample weights. With N training points, every point starts with weight \(w_i = 1/N\) — no point matters more than any other yet.
  2. Train a weak learner (a decision stump) on the data, using the current sample weights — the stump's split is chosen to minimize the weighted error, not the plain error.
  3. Compute its weighted error rate \(\epsilon_t\) — the sum of the weights of only the points it misclassified.
  4. Compute its "say" — the alpha weight. A better (lower-error) stump gets a bigger vote in the final ensemble.
  5. Increase the weights of the misclassified points (and decrease the correctly-classified ones), then renormalize so all weights sum to 1 again. The next stump now trains on data that's effectively "zoomed in" on the hard points.
  6. Repeat steps 2–5 for n_estimators rounds.
  7. Final prediction is a weighted vote of every stump trained, each one weighted by its own alpha.

The alpha ("say") formula

For a binary stump with weighted error rate \(\epsilon_t\):

$$ \alpha_t = \frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{1-\epsilon_t}{\epsilon_t}\right) $$

A stump with \(\epsilon_t = 0.5\) (no better than a coin flip) gets \(\alpha_t = 0\) — zero say, it contributes nothing. A stump with \(\epsilon_t \to 0\) gets \(\alpha_t \to \infty\) — an almost-perfect stump dominates the vote. A stump with \(\epsilon_t > 0.5\) (worse than random) gets a negative alpha — its vote is effectively inverted, since being reliably wrong is itself informative.

Weights are then updated point-by-point using that same alpha, then renormalized to sum to 1:

$$ w_i \leftarrow w_i \cdot e^{\alpha_t} \text{ if misclassified}, \qquad w_i \leftarrow w_i \cdot e^{-\alpha_t} \text{ if correct} $$

Worked example — 5 points, 2 rounds, real numbers

Five training points, all starting with weight \(1/5 = 0.2\).

RoundPoint weights going inStump misclassifiesε (weighted error)α (say)Point weights going out (normalized)
1 P1–P5: 0.20 each P3 only 0.20 0.5·ln(0.8/0.2) ≈ 0.693 P1,P2,P4,P5: 0.125 each · P3: 0.500
2 P1,P2,P4,P5: 0.125 · P3: 0.500 P1 and P5 0.125+0.125 = 0.25 0.5·ln(0.75/0.25) ≈ 0.549 P1: 0.250 · P2: 0.083 · P3: 0.333 · P4: 0.083 · P5: 0.250

Notice the story the weights tell: P3 was hard in round 1 (misclassified, weight jumps to 0.5), so round 2's stump is forced to pay attention to it — and it succeeds, correctly classifying P3, whose weight then drops back down to 0.333. But that stump has now started missing P1 and P5 instead, so their weights jump for round 3. Each round, the "hard region" of the data shifts, and the ensemble keeps chasing it.

If training stopped after round 2, the final prediction for any new point \(x\) would be a weighted vote: \(\text{sign}(\alpha_1 h_1(x) + \alpha_2 h_2(x)) = \text{sign}(0.693\,h_1(x) + 0.549\,h_2(x))\), where each \(h_t(x)\) is that round's stump prediction (+1/−1). Stump 1 gets slightly more say than stump 2, since it had the lower error rate.

Classification & Regression

Code — AdaBoostClassifier (and AdaBoostRegressor)

sklearn implements the whole reweighting loop for you — you only choose the base stump, how many rounds, and the learning rate.

from sklearn.ensemble import AdaBoostClassifier
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, random_state=1)

ada = AdaBoostClassifier(
    estimator=DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1),  # a "stump" — the classic weak learner
    n_estimators=100,
    learning_rate=1.0,
    algorithm='SAMME',   # multi-class-safe boosting algorithm (sklearn >= 1.6 default)
    random_state=1
)
ada.fit(X_train, y_train)
print("Test accuracy:", ada.score(X_test, y_test))

# Inspect how much "say" each stump got
print(ada.estimator_weights_[:5])   # the alpha_t values
print(ada.estimator_errors_[:5])    # the epsilon_t values

A regression version exists too — AdaBoostRegressor — using the same sequential-reweighting idea but with a loss-based weight update (linear/square/exponential loss on the residual) instead of the classification error rate.

from sklearn.ensemble import AdaBoostRegressor
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeRegressor

ada_reg = AdaBoostRegressor(
    estimator=DecisionTreeRegressor(max_depth=3),
    n_estimators=100,
    learning_rate=0.5,
    loss='linear',   # 'linear', 'square', or 'exponential'
    random_state=1
)
ada_reg.fit(X_train, y_train)
print("Test R^2:", ada_reg.score(X_test, y_test))

Hyperparameters & GridSearchCV

HyperparameterWhat it controlsPractical guidance
n_estimatorsNumber of sequential rounds (stumps) trainedUnlike Random Forest, more isn't always free — too many rounds on noisy data can eventually start overfitting, since later stumps chase increasingly noisy hard points. Tune it, don't just max it out.
learning_rateShrinks each stump's alpha contribution to the final vote by this factorThere's a direct tradeoff with n_estimators: a lower learning_rate needs more estimators to reach the same fit, but tends to generalize better (smaller, more cautious steps). A common strategy is to pick a small learning_rate (e.g. 0.05–0.5) and compensate with a larger n_estimators.
estimatorThe base weak learner (older sklearn versions call this base_estimator, now deprecated in favor of estimator)Default is a depth-1 stump. You can pass a slightly deeper tree (e.g. max_depth=2 or 3) for more expressive base learners, trading away some of AdaBoost's "many weak learners" character for fewer, stronger ones — usually stumps remain the standard choice.

n_estimators vs learning_rate — the shrinkage tradeoff

Think of learning_rate as "how much to trust each individual round" and n_estimators as "how many rounds you're allowed." A high learning rate with few estimators fits fast but can overshoot and overfit on the hardest (possibly mislabeled) points. A low learning rate with many estimators takes many small, cautious steps and usually generalizes better — at the cost of more training time. This exact tradeoff reappears, in a more famous form, in Gradient Boosting (next-next page).

GridSearchCV Example

from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV
from sklearn.ensemble import AdaBoostClassifier
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

param_grid = {
    'n_estimators': [50, 100, 200, 400],
    'learning_rate': [0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0],
    'estimator': [
        DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=1),
        DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2),
    ]
}
# 4 * 4 * 2 = 32 combinations, cv=5 -> 160 fits total

grid = GridSearchCV(
    estimator=AdaBoostClassifier(random_state=1),
    param_grid=param_grid,
    cv=5,
    scoring='accuracy',
    n_jobs=-1,
    verbose=1
)
grid.fit(X_train, y_train)

print("Best params:", grid.best_params_)
print("Best CV score:", grid.best_score_)
best_ada = grid.best_estimator_

Test Your Understanding

Hard

5 questions on AdaBoost. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. A decision stump has a weighted error rate of exactly 0.5 on the current sample weights. What alpha (say) does it receive, and what does that mean?

Explanation: α_t = 0.5·ln((1-ε)/ε). At ε=0.5, (1-ε)/ε = 1, and ln(1)=0, so α=0. A coin-flip-accurate stump is genuinely worthless information and correctly gets zero weight in the vote.

2. In the 5-point worked example, after round 1 point P3 (the misclassified point) has its weight increased to 0.5 — half the total weight mass on a single point out of five. What is the direct consequence for round 2's stump?

Explanation: The stump's split is chosen to minimize the weighted error rate. With P3 carrying half the total weight, getting P3 right (or wrong) dominates that error calculation — this is precisely the mechanism by which boosting "focuses" the next learner on previous mistakes.

3. Why does AdaBoost traditionally use decision stumps (depth-1 trees) rather than deep trees as its base learner?

Explanation: Boosting is built to turn many weak learners into one strong learner. Weak, high-bias stumps leave plenty of "room" for each new round to meaningfully correct remaining errors. Deep, already-low-bias trees would leave little left to correct and would compound overfitting risk across rounds.

4. You lower learning_rate from 1.0 to 0.1 but keep n_estimators fixed at 100. What is the most likely immediate effect?

Explanation: learning_rate scales down each round's alpha contribution to the final vote. With fewer, smaller contributions and the same round budget, the ensemble hasn't had "enough steps" to fully fit — the classic n_estimators/learning_rate tradeoff.

5. Your training data has a handful of mislabeled outliers. Compared to Random Forest, why is AdaBoost typically more vulnerable to being thrown off by them?

Explanation: This is the core noise-sensitivity tradeoff of boosting: reweighting has no mechanism to distinguish "genuinely hard pattern" from "mislabeled noise" — both keep getting upweighted round after round. Bagging/Random Forest's parallel, independent trees have no such reinforcing loop, making them comparatively robust to a few bad labels.
