40. Hyperparameter Tuning with Optuna

Why Bayesian optimization beats Grid/Random Search · studies, trials & objectives · a full worked example · pruning bad trials early

Extended notes — this topic isn't covered in the CampusX playlist notes this site is based on. Written from scratch to continue the curriculum, in the same style as the rest of the site.
Concept

Why Optuna over GridSearchCV / RandomizedSearchCV

Back on the Random Forest page you met sklearn's two standard search tools. Both have a shared blind spot worth naming directly: neither one learns anything from the trials it has already run. Grid search marches through every combination in a fixed list regardless of how earlier combinations scored. Random search samples blindly from a distribution, also regardless of earlier results. Both treat every trial as if it were the first trial ever run.

GridSearchCV

Exhaustive — tries every combination in your grid. Guaranteed to find the best point in the grid, but the number of combinations multiplies with every added parameter or value — combinatorial explosion.

RandomizedSearchCV

Samples n_iter random combinations. Cost is capped directly by n_iter regardless of how many parameters you search — a real improvement — but each sample is still drawn blind, ignoring what earlier trials revealed.

Optuna

Uses the results of every previous trial to intelligently choose where to sample next — by default via a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE), a form of Bayesian optimization. It converges to good hyperparameters in noticeably fewer trials than blind search.

The practical payoff: for an expensive model (deep neural nets, large gradient-boosted ensembles, anything where a single training run takes minutes to hours), the difference between "100 blind random trials" and "50 trials that each build on what was learned from the last" can be the difference between a tuning run that finishes overnight and one that doesn't finish at all.

Concept

Bayesian Optimization — The Intuition

You don't need the underlying probability math to use Optuna well — the intuition is what matters, and it's genuinely simple once stated plainly.

Analogy — tasting a new dish by adjusting the recipe

Imagine you're trying to find the best amount of salt and cook time for a recipe, and each attempt costs you an hour of cooking. After a few attempts, you start noticing patterns: "too much salt and short cook time tasted bad," "moderate salt with long cook time tasted great." You wouldn't randomly try amounts on your next attempt — you'd focus near the combination that tasted great, while occasionally trying something a little different in case there's an even better combination you haven't discovered yet. That's Bayesian optimization in one sentence: build a running belief about which regions of the hyperparameter space tend to score well, then use that belief to choose the next point to try.

Exploitation vs. Exploration

Every step, the optimizer balances two competing instincts:

  • Exploitation — try hyperparameters close to the best ones found so far, since that region has already shown it scores well.
  • Exploration — try hyperparameters in a region that hasn't been sampled much yet, since it's still uncertain whether that region might actually be even better.

Pure exploitation risks getting stuck at a locally-good-but-not-globally-best combination (never checking if something wildly different works better). Pure exploration is just random search again, wasting trials in regions already known to be mediocre. TPE (and Bayesian optimization generally) models the trade-off explicitly, weighing "how good do we expect this region to be" against "how uncertain are we about this region" when choosing the next trial.

Under the hood, TPE specifically models "the distribution of hyperparameters that led to good scores" separately from "the distribution of hyperparameters that led to bad scores," and picks the next candidate that looks relatively more likely under the good distribution than the bad one. You don't need to implement any of this — Optuna's default sampler handles it — but it's worth knowing that "TPE" isn't a mysterious black box, just a specific, practical way of formalizing the salt-and-cook-time intuition above.

Concept

Core Optuna Concepts

Optuna's whole API rests on four ideas that fit together tightly. Understanding these four is basically understanding the entire library.

ConceptWhat it is
StudyAn entire optimization run — a collection of trials working toward maximizing or minimizing one objective. Created with optuna.create_study().
TrialOne single attempt — one specific set of hyperparameter values, trained and scored once. A study runs many trials.
Objective functionA function you write, taking a trial object as its argument. Inside it, you ask the trial to suggest hyperparameter values, train/evaluate a model with them, and return a single score.
trial.suggest_*Calls inside your objective function — trial.suggest_float(...), trial.suggest_int(...), trial.suggest_categorical(...) — that ask Optuna's sampler for the next value to try for one hyperparameter, given everything learned from previous trials.

The overall flow: define an objective function → create a study → call study.optimize(objective, n_trials=...) → Optuna repeatedly calls your objective with different trial objects, using its sampler to choose increasingly promising hyperparameters each time → read off study.best_params and study.best_value at the end.

Classification & Regression

Full Worked Example — Tuning a RandomForestClassifier

Same dataset, same model family you tuned with GridSearchCV/RandomizedSearchCV on the Random Forest page — now tuned with Optuna, using cross-validated accuracy as the score to maximize.

import optuna
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, StratifiedKFold

def objective(trial):
    # Ask the trial to suggest values for the hyperparameters we're tuning.
    # Optuna's sampler picks each value using what it has learned from past trials.
    n_estimators = trial.suggest_int('n_estimators', 50, 500)
    max_depth = trial.suggest_int('max_depth', 2, 32)
    max_features = trial.suggest_float('max_features', 0.1, 1.0)
    min_samples_leaf = trial.suggest_int('min_samples_leaf', 1, 10)
    criterion = trial.suggest_categorical('criterion', ['gini', 'entropy'])

    model = RandomForestClassifier(
        n_estimators=n_estimators,
        max_depth=max_depth,
        max_features=max_features,
        min_samples_leaf=min_samples_leaf,
        criterion=criterion,
        n_jobs=-1,
        random_state=1
    )

    cv = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=1)
    scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=cv, scoring='accuracy', n_jobs=-1)

    return scores.mean()   # Optuna will try to MAXIMIZE this returned value

# Create a study telling Optuna whether to maximize or minimize the objective
study = optuna.create_study(direction='maximize')

# Run 50 trials -- each one calls objective() with a new trial object
study.optimize(objective, n_trials=50)

print("Best hyperparameters:", study.best_params)
print("Best CV accuracy:", study.best_value)

# Train the final model on the full training set using the best found hyperparameters
best_model = RandomForestClassifier(**study.best_params, n_jobs=-1, random_state=1)
best_model.fit(X_train, y_train)
print("Test accuracy:", best_model.score(X_test, y_test))

Walkthrough

  1. objective(trial) is called once per trial. Every trial.suggest_* call asks the sampler for one value, informed by all prior trials' results.
  2. A fresh RandomForestClassifier is built with those suggested values and scored with 5-fold cross-validation, exactly as you would when manually tuning.
  3. The function returns the mean CV accuracy — this single number is what Optuna is trying to optimize.
  4. direction='maximize' tells the study that higher returned values are better (use 'minimize' for something like RMSE or log-loss).
  5. study.optimize(objective, n_trials=50) runs the loop 50 times, each time using TPE to choose increasingly promising hyperparameter combinations.
  6. study.best_params and study.best_value give you the winning hyperparameters and the score they achieved — ready to retrain a final model with.

The same idea works for XGBoost, or any model at all

Optuna doesn't care what's inside your objective function — it's completely model-agnostic. Swapping the Random Forest for XGBoost just means suggesting different hyperparameters:

import xgboost as xgb

def objective(trial):
    params = {
        'n_estimators': trial.suggest_int('n_estimators', 100, 1000),
        'max_depth': trial.suggest_int('max_depth', 2, 10),
        'learning_rate': trial.suggest_float('learning_rate', 1e-3, 0.3, log=True),
        'subsample': trial.suggest_float('subsample', 0.5, 1.0),
        'colsample_bytree': trial.suggest_float('colsample_bytree', 0.5, 1.0),
    }
    model = xgb.XGBClassifier(**params, use_label_encoder=False, eval_metric='logloss', random_state=1)
    scores = cross_val_score(model, X_train, y_train, cv=5, scoring='accuracy', n_jobs=-1)
    return scores.mean()

study = optuna.create_study(direction='maximize')
study.optimize(objective, n_trials=50)
print(study.best_params)

Notice log=True on learning_rate — for hyperparameters that naturally span orders of magnitude (0.001 to 0.3), sampling on a log scale explores small and large values more evenly than a plain linear range would.

Concept

Pruning — Stopping Bad Trials Early

For models trained iteratively (neural networks over epochs, gradient boosting over boosting rounds), you often don't need to wait until the very end of training to know a trial is going badly — its intermediate performance already looks clearly worse than other trials at the same point. Pruning lets Optuna kill such trials early, freeing up compute for more promising ones instead of wasting time finishing a run that was never going to be competitive.

MedianPruner — the common default

At each intermediate checkpoint (e.g. after every training epoch), a trial reports its current score via trial.report(score, step). MedianPruner compares that intermediate score against the median of all other trials' scores at the same checkpoint — if this trial is doing worse than the median of its peers at this same point, it gets pruned (stopped) rather than run to completion.

import optuna

def objective(trial):
    lr = trial.suggest_float('learning_rate', 1e-4, 1e-1, log=True)
    model = build_model(lr)   # your model-building code

    for epoch in range(20):
        train_one_epoch(model)
        val_score = evaluate(model)

        trial.report(val_score, step=epoch)   # report intermediate progress

        if trial.should_prune():               # ask Optuna: is this trial worth continuing?
            raise optuna.TrialPruned()          # stop early -- don't waste compute on a losing trial

    return val_score

study = optuna.create_study(
    direction='maximize',
    pruner=optuna.pruners.MedianPruner(n_startup_trials=5, n_warmup_steps=3)
)
study.optimize(objective, n_trials=50)

n_startup_trials lets the first few trials run without pruning (there's no meaningful median yet), and n_warmup_steps gives each individual trial a few checkpoints of grace before it becomes eligible for pruning — avoiding killing a trial that just started slow but would have caught up.

Test Your Understanding

Hard

5 questions on Optuna & Hyperparameter Tuning. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. What is the core weakness shared by both GridSearchCV and RandomizedSearchCV that Optuna's default sampler directly addresses?

Explanation: Grid search enumerates a fixed grid and random search samples blindly — neither adapts based on which combinations scored well or poorly. Optuna's default TPE sampler explicitly builds a model of good vs. bad regions from past trial results and uses it to choose the next candidate, converging faster.

2. In the exploration vs. exploitation tradeoff, what does "exploration" mean in the context of Bayesian hyperparameter optimization?

Explanation: Exploration means sampling in less-certain regions of the hyperparameter space to check whether an even-better combination exists there, balanced against exploitation (focusing near already-known good regions). Pure exploitation risks getting stuck at a local optimum; pure exploration wastes trials and degenerates into random search.

3. In an Optuna objective function, what must the function return, and what does study.optimize actually do with it?

Explanation: The objective function's single returned number (e.g. mean cross-validated accuracy) is exactly what the study is trying to maximize or minimize (per the direction argument). Every trial's (hyperparameters, returned score) pair feeds the sampler's model of "which regions tend to score well," which is how later trials get chosen more intelligently.

4. You're tuning a neural network's learning rate across a range from 0.0001 to 0.5 using trial.suggest_float. Why would you set log=True for this parameter?

Explanation: With a plain linear range from 0.0001 to 0.5, a uniform sampler would rarely land near the small end (0.0001–0.001), even though small learning rates are often exactly where the interesting behavior is. log=True samples uniformly in log-space instead, giving small and large values comparable coverage — standard practice for learning rates, regularization strengths, and similar parameters that matter across orders of magnitude.

5. A trial is being trained epoch-by-epoch and calls trial.report(val_score, step=epoch) after every epoch, with a MedianPruner attached to the study. What does should_prune() effectively check?

Explanation: MedianPruner compares a trial's reported intermediate score, at a given step, against the median of all other trials' reported scores at that same step. If it's falling clearly behind the pack this early, should_prune() returns True and the trial can be stopped (via raising optuna.TrialPruned()) rather than wasting further compute training a run that's very unlikely to end up competitive.
