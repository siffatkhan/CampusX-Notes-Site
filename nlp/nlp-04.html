4. Word2Vec & Text Classification

Dense semantic embeddings · CBOW vs Skip-gram · Cosine similarity · The text classification pipeline

NLP

What Is Word Embedding?

Word Embedding is the umbrella term for any technique that converts text into numerical vectors, so machines (which only understand numbers) can process language. All the text representation techniques from chapter 3, plus Word2Vec, fall under this umbrella — they split into two families:

Frequency-based — count how often words appear: Bag of Words, TF-IDF, GloVe (based on matrix factorization of word co-occurrence counts).
Prediction-based — train a neural network to predict something, and use the learned weights as vectors: Word2Vec.

Old techniques like BoW/TF-IDF just count occurrences — they have no understanding of meaning. "Happy" and "joy" are treated as completely unrelated, just like "happy" and "table" would be. Word2Vec fixes three major problems at once:

1. Captures semantic meaning — "happy" and "joy" end up close together in vector space.
2. Low-dimensional — compact vectors (commonly 100–300 dims) instead of a vector as long as the whole vocabulary → much faster computation.
3. Dense — most values are non-zero (unlike sparse BoW/TF-IDF), which generalizes better and reduces overfitting.
Analogy: BoW/TF-IDF describes a person only by which exact words appear on their ID card — very rigid, no notion of similarity. Word2Vec describes a person using a rich profile of traits (personality, interests, habits) — so two people can be recognized as "similar" even if their ID cards look nothing alike.

The Core Intuition Behind Word2Vec

The single most important idea: words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. This is the Distributional Hypothesis — "a word is known by the company it keeps." If "king" and "queen" often appear surrounded by similar neighboring words (royal, throne, crown, palace), the model learns they must be semantically related — without ever being told what "royal" means.

To build intuition, imagine manually describing words using human-understandable features — gender, wealth, power, whether it speaks:

Each word becomes a point in an n-dimensional space defined by these feature values; words with similar values sit close together geometrically. The catch: we don't manually design these features — that's impossible to do for every nuance of language. Instead a neural network automatically discovers these features by solving a prediction task, and the numbers it lands on become each word's vector. We don't even know what each dimension "means" — the network invents its own abstract features that happen to organize words meaningfully.

NLP

How Word2Vec Actually Creates Vectors

Word2Vec uses a shallow neural network trained on a fake/dummy task — the real goal isn't the task itself, but the byproduct: the weight matrix learned along the way becomes the word vectors.

Input (one-hot word vector) → Hidden Layer (no activation, just weights) → Output Layer (softmax over vocabulary)

  • Input layer size = vocabulary size V — each word represented as a one-hot vector.
  • Hidden layer size = N (the embedding dimension you choose, e.g. 100 or 300).
  • The weight matrix between input and hidden layer (size V × N) is exactly what becomes the word embeddings — each row corresponds to one word's vector.

Once training finishes, the output layer is thrown away entirely — only the hidden weight matrix matters. That's the "byproduct" the model was really after.

Analogy: like training someone to guess a person's job by only observing their daily habits (context). We don't actually care about the "job-guessing" skill — what we care about is the rich internal understanding of "types of people" that they develop while learning to guess. Word2Vec similarly doesn't care about its literal prediction task; it cares about the internal representation (weights) built while solving it.
NLP

Cosine Similarity

Once every word is a vector: how do we check if two words are similar? We don't compare raw numbers directly — we measure the angle between two vectors.

$$ \text{cosine similarity} = \cos(\theta) = \frac{A \cdot B}{\|A\| \, \|B\|} $$

Range: −1 to 1. 1 → same direction → highly similar words (e.g. "happy" & "joy"). 0 → perpendicular → unrelated. −1 → opposite directions → opposite meaning.

Why cosine and not Euclidean distance? Cosine similarity only cares about direction, not magnitude/length. Two vectors can point the exact same way but have different lengths (e.g. a word that appeared more often in training gets a "longer" vector) — cosine correctly still calls them similar, while raw distance metrics could be misled by the length difference.

Analogy: two arrows on a map, both pointing exactly northeast — one short, one long. Cosine similarity says "these point the same direction, same idea," ignoring how long each arrow is. Euclidean distance would instead focus on how far apart their tips are — misleading here.

This is the standard way embeddings are compared — used for synonyms, nearest neighbors, and word analogies like king − man + woman ≈ queen.

Two Architectures: CBOW vs. Skip-gram

Both use a sliding window of context around a target word — this is the "fake problem" being solved.

CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words)

Task: given surrounding context words, predict the target (center) word. Sentence "The quick brown fox jumps," target "brown": Input = context words quick, fox → Output = predict brown.

Analogy: a fill-in-the-blank quiz — "The quick ___ fox jumps" — you use the surrounding words to guess the missing one.

Skip-gram

Task: the exact reverse — given the target word, predict the context words around it. Target "brown" → Input = brown → Output = predict quick, fox.

Analogy: shown "brown" and asked "what words usually surround this one?" — like guessing it might appear near "fox," "bear," "hair."

Complete Skip-gram Example, Step by Step

Sentence: "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog." Window size = 2. Target word: "fox." Context words within window: quick, brown, jumps, over.

Skip-gram creates training pairs (input = target, output = one context word each):

(fox → quick)
(fox → brown)
(fox → jumps)
(fox → over)

Each pair is fed to the network: Input "fox" (one-hot, size V) → Hidden Layer (size N=300, no activation — this row = fox's embedding) → Output Layer (softmax over vocab V) tries to predict quick / brown / jumps / over.

The network is trained to maximize the probability of predicting real context words correctly. Words like "fox" that repeatedly co-occur with "quick," "jumps," "animal," "wild," etc. across millions of sentences gradually get pushed toward similar hidden-layer weight patterns as other animal-related words ("dog," "cat," "wolf") — because they share similar context distributions.

After training on the full corpus, the output layer is discarded. The input→hidden weight matrix (V × N) is kept — the row for "fox" is fox's final embedding vector.

When to Use Which

CBOWSkip-gram
PredictsTarget word from contextContext words from target

Training speedFaster (fewer predictions per window)Slower (predicts multiple context words per target)
Quality on rare wordsWeakerBetter — each word gets multiple training examples (one per context word)
Improving Word2Vec embeddings
  • Increase training data — richer, more reliable context patterns
  • Increase vector dimensions — more nuance, but too high risks sparsity/overfitting again (sweet spot ≈ 100–300)
  • Increase window size — small (e.g. 2) captures syntactic/grammar-like closeness; large (e.g. 5–10) captures topical/semantic similarity
OOV & the FastText fix

Word2Vec only produces vectors for words seen during training — "dragonfruit" never appearing means vector("dragonfruit") simply fails, no fallback. FastText represents each word as character n-grams (e.g. <dr, dra, rag, …, uit>) so it can build a vector for an unseen word from familiar sub-word pieces — handling rare words, misspellings, and morphological variants.

Pretrained vs self-trained: a pretrained model (e.g. trained on Google News, ~100B words) is fastest to use, but works best when your data's vocabulary overlaps at least ~80% with the pretrained vocabulary. A self-trained model (via Gensim) is better for specialized domains (medical, legal) but needs enough of your own data. Word2Vec's key limitation to remember: it's a static embedding — "bank" always gets the same vector regardless of sentence, so it can't handle polysemy the way BERT can.

Text Classification

Text classification is a supervised task — the same general idea as classification on tabular data or images, applied to text. Example: is this email spam or not spam?

Binary — 2 classes (spam / not spam).
Multiclass — more than 2 classes (e.g. news topic: sports / politics / tech).
Multi-label — a single input can belong to multiple classes at once (a news article can be both "sports" and "politics").
The pipeline (per source): Data acquisition → Text preprocessing → Text vectorization (BoW, TF-IDF, Word2Vec) → Modeling (e.g. Naive Bayes) → Evaluation (accuracy, confusion matrix) → Deploy. Approaches range across the same spectrum as the rest of NLP: Heuristics → APIs → ML → DL.

Why Naive Bayes and Logistic Regression are the classic default choices: they're fast, work well on high-dimensional sparse vectors (exactly what TF-IDF/BoW produce), and are easy to interpret. SVM is a strong alternative when classes are cleanly separable in vector space. Neural network classifiers become worthwhile once you have large datasets and/or want to feed in dense Word2Vec/BERT embeddings instead of sparse counts.

Worked Example — TF-IDF + Naive Bayes

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, confusion_matrix

# X = raw (preprocessed) text column, y = labels (e.g. spam/not-spam)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    df['text'], df['label'], test_size=0.2, random_state=42
)

# 1. Vectorize — fit only on training data to avoid leakage
tfidf = TfidfVectorizer(max_features=5000)
X_train_vec = tfidf.fit_transform(X_train)
X_test_vec = tfidf.transform(X_test)

# 2. Train a classifier
model = MultinomialNB()
model.fit(X_train_vec, y_train)

# 3. Evaluate
y_pred = model.predict(X_test_vec)
print("Accuracy:", accuracy_score(y_test, y_pred))
print(confusion_matrix(y_test, y_pred))

# 4. Predict on new text
new_text = ["Congratulations! You won a free prize, click now"]
new_vec = tfidf.transform(new_text)
print(model.predict(new_vec))   # e.g. ['spam']

Swap MultinomialNB() for LogisticRegression() to try a different classifier on the exact same features — the pipeline shape (vectorize → fit → predict → evaluate) stays identical, which is why classic text classification is often the fastest first baseline to build for a new NLP problem.

Test Your Understanding

Hard

5 questions on Word2Vec & Text Classification. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. A team has a very large training corpus and cares especially about getting good embeddings for rare technical terms. Which Word2Vec architecture should they prefer, and why?

Explanation: Per the CBOW vs Skip-gram comparison table — Skip-gram is best for large datasets and produces better quality on rare words, since each occurrence generates multiple training examples (one per context word), unlike CBOW's single prediction per window.

2. After training a Word2Vec model, why is the output (softmax) layer thrown away and only the input→hidden weight matrix kept?

Explanation: Word2Vec's prediction task (CBOW/skip-gram) is a "fake problem" — the real value is the input→hidden weight matrix (V×N), where each row is a word's embedding. The output layer was only needed during training to produce a gradient signal.

3. Two word vectors point in exactly the same direction, but one is much longer than the other (e.g. because that word appeared more often during training). What does cosine similarity report, and why is this the right behavior?

Explanation: Cosine similarity measures the angle between vectors, not their length — two same-direction vectors of different lengths still score close to 1, correctly reflecting that they represent similar meanings, unlike Euclidean distance which would be misled by the length gap.

4. A single news article is tagged as belonging to both "Sports" and "Politics" simultaneously (e.g. a story about a player's political endorsement). What type of text classification is this?

Explanation: Per the source's own example — a single news article that can belong to sports AND politics simultaneously is exactly multi-label classification, distinct from multiclass (exactly one class chosen from several) or binary (two mutually exclusive classes).

5. In the TF-IDF + Naive Bayes code example, why is TfidfVectorizer fit only on X_train and then just .transform()-ed (not re-fit) on X_test?

Explanation: This is the standard train/test discipline (echoed from the ML track): fitting the vectorizer only on training data prevents the test set's statistics from leaking into the features the model is evaluated on, keeping the evaluation honest about generalization to truly unseen data.
