5. Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging

Assigning grammatical roles to words · spaCy tagging · Hidden Markov Models · The Viterbi algorithm

NLP

What Is POS Tagging?

Definition: POS tagging means assigning the correct grammatical category (noun, verb, adjective, pronoun, etc.) to every word in a sentence — based on both the word itself and the context it's used in, since the same word can be a different part of speech depending on its role in the sentence (e.g. "book" as a noun — "I am reading a book" — versus "book" as a verb — "I will book a flight").

Sentence: "I am Saifat"
Tags: PRON VERB PROPN

POS tagging is a preprocessing step — not usually the final goal by itself, but a foundation that other, more complex NLP applications depend on.

Analogy: like labeling every piece of furniture in a house as "chair," "table," "lamp," before you can give instructions like "move the chair near the table." You need to know what category each word belongs to before you can reason about how they relate to each other in a sentence.

Why Do We Need POS Tagging? (Applications)

Named Entity Recognition (NER)

Identifying names of people, organizations, locations relies heavily on knowing which words are proper nouns (PROPN) vs regular words — e.g. "Apple" (ORG) vs "apple" (fruit, common noun). Full coverage in chapter 6.

Question Answering Systems

To answer "Who wrote this book?" the system needs to identify nouns/proper nouns as candidate answers, and verbs to understand the action being asked about.

Word Sense Disambiguation

The same word used in different grammatical roles conveys different meanings — POS tagging is often the first clue used to resolve this ambiguity.

Chatbots

Understanding sentence structure (who's doing what to whom) requires knowing each word's grammatical role, helping the bot correctly parse user intent.

Analogy: without POS tags, a sentence is just a bag of words to a machine. With POS tags, the machine starts to see structure — like the difference between reading random letters versus words organized into a sentence.
NLP

Using spaCy for POS Tagging

import spacy
nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")

doc = nlp("I am Saifat")

for token in doc:
    print(token.text, token.pos_, token.tag_)

spaCy gives two levels of granularity:

token.pos_ — coarse-grained POS: the broad category (NOUN, VERB, PRON, ADJ, PROPN, etc.).
token.tag_ — fine-grained POS: much more detailed grammatical information, e.g. distinguishing VBP (verb, non-3rd person singular present) vs VBD (verb, past tense) vs VBG (verb, gerund/present participle). "am" might be coarse = AUX/VERB, fine = VBP.
Analogy: coarse-grained tag = saying "it's a vehicle." Fine-grained tag = saying "it's a 2-door manual sedan." Both are true, but the fine-grained tag gives much more precise, useful detail.

Visualizing with displaCy

spaCy's companion library displaCy lets you visually render POS tags and dependency structure as a diagram instead of reading raw text output.

from spacy import displacy
displacy.render(doc, style="dep")
NLP

How POS Tagging Actually Works: Hidden Markov Models

Given a sentence, we want to find the most likely sequence of POS tags for those words. This is fundamentally a sequence labeling problem — the tag for a word doesn't just depend on the word itself, it also depends on what tags came before it (grammar has structure — a determiner like "the" is very likely followed by a noun or adjective, not another determiner).

Analogy: predicting tomorrow's weather. You don't just look at "what usually happens on this date" (the word alone) — you also consider "what was the weather yesterday" (the previous tag), since weather tends to follow patterns. HMM applies this same "previous state affects next state" logic to grammar.

An HMM is built from a training corpus (a large set of sentences already tagged by humans). From it we calculate two types of probabilities:

Transition Probability

Given the current POS tag, how likely is the next word to have a certain POS tag? Captures grammar patterns — e.g. how likely a NOUN is to be followed by a VERB. Special <START>/<END> markers are added to every sentence to model which tag typically starts/ends one.

Calculated by dividing by the row sum — count how many times tag A is followed by tag B, divided by the total times tag A is followed by anything.

$$ P(\text{tag}_i \mid \text{tag}_{i-1}) = \frac{\text{count(tag}_{i-1} \text{ followed by tag}_i)}{\text{count(tag}_{i-1}\text{ followed by anything)}} $$

Emission Probability

Given a POS tag, how likely is a specific word to be generated from that tag? Captures word-tag association — e.g. how likely "dog" is to appear when the tag is NOUN.

Calculated by dividing by the column sum — count how many times a word appears with a specific tag, divided by the total times that tag appeared at all.

$$ P(\text{word}_i \mid \text{tag}_i) = \frac{\text{count(word}_i \text{ tagged as tag}_i)}{\text{count(tag}_i \text{ appearing at all)}} $$

Together: transition probability = "grammar sense" (what usually comes next grammatically); emission probability = "vocabulary sense" (what word usually shows up for this tag).

Full Worked Example

Tiny tagged training corpus:

<START> The/DET dog/NOUN barks/VERB <END>
<START> The/DET cat/NOUN runs/VERB <END>
<START> A/DET dog/NOUN sleeps/VERB <END>

Step 1 — Transition probabilities (divide each row by its row sum): P(DET|START) = 3/3 = 1.0, P(NOUN|DET) = 3/3 = 1.0, P(VERB|NOUN) = 3/3 = 1.0, P(END|VERB) = 3/3 = 1.0. (A real, larger corpus would spread these across multiple tags instead of a clean 1.0 — this toy example is simplified.)

Step 2 — Emission probabilities (divide each column by its column sum): P(The|DET) = 2/3 ≈ 0.67, P(A|DET) = 1/3 ≈ 0.33, P(dog|NOUN) = 2/3 ≈ 0.67, P(cat|NOUN) = 1/3 ≈ 0.33, P(barks|VERB) = 1/3 ≈ 0.33.

Step 3 — Tag a new sentence: "The dog runs", sequence DET → NOUN → VERB:

$$ P(\text{DET}\!\to\!\text{NOUN}\!\to\!\text{VERB}) = P(\text{DET}|\text{START}) \times P(\text{The}|\text{DET}) \times P(\text{NOUN}|\text{DET}) \times P(\text{dog}|\text{NOUN}) \times P(\text{VERB}|\text{NOUN}) \times P(\text{runs}|\text{VERB}) $$ $$ = 1.0 \times 0.67 \times 1.0 \times 0.67 \times 1.0 \times 0.33 \approx 0.148 $$

This is the probability of one specific tag sequence through the sentence. In a real sentence, each word could take multiple possible tags, creating many possible paths — and we need to efficiently find the single best one.

NLP

The Viterbi Algorithm

For a sentence with n words and a tagset with T possible tags, there are Tn possible tag sequences to consider — trying every combination is computationally explosive. For a 10-word sentence with 20 possible tags, that's 20¹⁰ ≈ 10 trillion combinations, completely infeasible to brute-force. This is exactly what "optimization" means here: find the single most probable tag sequence without computing the probability of every possible sequence one by one.

Analogy: finding the shortest driving route from City A to City Z through intermediate cities with multiple connecting roads. Checking every full route combination is enormously wasteful. Instead, at each intermediate city, only keep track of the best way to reach that city so far — discard all worse paths reaching that same city. This is exactly the idea behind Viterbi.

The Viterbi algorithm is a dynamic programming algorithm: instead of recomputing probabilities for every full path from scratch, it builds up the solution word by word, and at each step, for each possible tag, keeps only the single best (highest-probability) path leading to that tag at that position.

  1. Initialization — for the first word, compute the probability of each possible tag using P(tag | START) × P(word | tag).
  2. Recursion (core step) — for each subsequent word and each possible tag, compute the best score to reach that tag from any previous tag, keeping only the maximum:
    $$ \text{best\_score}(tag_i) = \max_{prev\_tag} \left[ \text{best\_score}(prev\_tag) \times P(tag_i \mid prev\_tag) \right] \times P(word_i \mid tag_i) $$
  3. Store backpointers — remember which previous tag gave the best score, so the full best path can be reconstructed at the end.
  4. Termination — at the last word, pick the tag with the highest overall score, then backtrack through the stored backpointers to recover the entire best tag sequence.

Why this is efficient: instead of Tn total paths, Viterbi only ever needs to track T possible "best scores" at each of the n word positions — reducing complexity from exponential (Tn) down to roughly polynomial (n × T²). This is what makes POS tagging on real sentences computationally practical.

Putting It All Together

Training Corpus (tagged sentences)
↓
Transition Probabilities (grammar) + Emission Probabilities (vocabulary)
↓
New Sentence to Tag
↓
Viterbi Algorithm (dynamic programming)
↓
Most Likely POS Tag Sequence

One-line intuition: HMM defines how likely any given tag sequence is (via transition + emission probabilities); Viterbi is the efficient search strategy that finds the best sequence without checking every possibility.

Test Your Understanding

Hard

5 questions on POS Tagging. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. "I will book a flight" vs "I am reading a book" — why does POS tagging need context, not just the word itself, to tag "book" correctly?

Explanation: This is the source's own example — "book" as verb (to reserve) vs "book" as noun (an object). POS tagging assigns tags based on both the word and its role/context, which is exactly why the same word can get different tags in different sentences.

2. Which is the correct distinction between transition probability and emission probability in an HMM POS tagger?

Explanation: Transition probability (row sum) captures "what tag usually follows this tag" — grammar. Emission probability (column sum) captures "what word usually appears for this tag" — vocabulary. Both are calculated from the same labeled training corpus.

3. Why is brute-force tag-sequence search infeasible for a 10-word sentence with 20 possible tags?

Explanation: With T possible tags and n words, there are T^n possible sequences — for T=20, n=10, that's roughly 10 trillion combinations, exactly the exponential blow-up the notes describe as "completely infeasible to brute-force."

4. What is the core trick that lets Viterbi avoid the exponential blow-up of brute-force search?

Explanation: Viterbi's dynamic-programming insight: for each tag at each word position, only the single best (highest-probability) path reaching it is kept — all worse alternatives are discarded immediately — reducing complexity from T^n down to roughly n×T².

5. What is the difference between spaCy's token.pos_ and token.tag_?

Explanation: pos_ gives the coarse-grained category (NOUN, VERB, PRON, …); tag_ gives fine-grained detail like tense/form (VBP, VBD, VBG) — the "vehicle" vs "2-door manual sedan" analogy from the notes.
