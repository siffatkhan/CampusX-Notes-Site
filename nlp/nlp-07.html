7. Topic Modeling

Discovering hidden themes across a document collection · Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) · Unsupervised, no labels required

NLP

What Is Topic Modeling?

Topic Modeling is an unsupervised learning technique that automatically discovers the abstract "topics" running through a collection of documents — without any labeled data, and without being told in advance what the topics even are.

Feed in thousands of news articles → topic modeling might automatically discover: Topic 1: election, government, president, vote → "Politics" Topic 2: goal, match, team, player → "Sports" Topic 3: stock, market, economy, inflation → "Finance"

The algorithm never explicitly labels these clusters "Politics" or "Sports" — it only groups statistically co-occurring words into clusters. A human looks at each cluster's top words afterward and assigns the human-readable label.

Analogy: imagine dumping thousands of unsorted newspaper clippings on a table with no headings or categories. Topic modeling is an assistant who reads through all of them and starts making piles based on which clippings tend to use similar vocabulary — one pile naturally becomes "sports" clippings, another "politics," without ever being told those category names in advance. You only look at each pile afterward and name it based on what's in it.

Why Topic Modeling? — Use Cases

Organizing Large Document Collections

Automatically categorize thousands of articles, support tickets, or reviews into themes without manual tagging — useful the moment a collection is too large for a human to sort by hand.

Trend Discovery

See what themes are emerging across large volumes of text — e.g. social media posts — as they shift over time, without reading every post.

Content Recommendation

Group articles or products by discovered topic so a recommendation system can suggest similar content, beyond just keyword or category matching.

Summarizing Large Corpora

Quickly understand "what is this huge pile of documents mostly about" — e.g. discovering the recurring themes in a mountain of customer feedback or product reviews — without reading everything.

Key Difference From Everything Covered So Far

This is fundamentally different from POS tagging (chapter 5) and NER (chapter 6):

  • POS tagging / NER = supervised — trained on labeled examples where humans already tagged the correct answer.
  • Topic Modeling = unsupervised — no labels at all; the algorithm discovers structure purely from patterns of word co-occurrence across documents.
Analogy: POS tagging / NER is a student learning from an answer key (supervised). Topic modeling is a student left alone with a huge stack of books and asked to group them by theme purely by noticing which books share similar vocabulary — no answer key provided at all (unsupervised).

Core Intuition: How Does It Work?

The foundational assumption behind topic modeling: each document is a mixture of multiple topics, and each topic is a distribution over words.

  • A single document isn't purely "100% about sports" — it might be 70% Sports, 20% Business (e.g. an article about a player's contract negotiation), 10% something else.
  • A "topic" itself isn't one fixed keyword — it's a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary, where certain words are much more likely to appear under that topic than others.
Topic "Sports" as a word distribution: goal → 0.08 match → 0.07 team → 0.06 player → 0.05 election → 0.0001 (extremely unlikely)
Document: "The player negotiated a new contract worth millions before the match." Topic mixture: 60% Sports, 35% Business/Finance, 5% Other

The algorithm's job is to work backward: given only the raw text of many documents, figure out (a) what the underlying topics (word distributions) probably are, and (b) what mixture of those topics each document is made of — purely from statistical word co-occurrence patterns.

NLP

Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA)

LDA is the most widely used topic modeling algorithm. Its name breaks down exactly what it does:

"Latent"

The topics are hidden / not directly observed — we only ever see the words, and must infer the topics that likely generated them.

"Dirichlet"

The type of probability distribution used to model "how topics mix within documents" and "how words mix within topics." A Dirichlet distribution is well-suited for modeling proportions that must sum to 1, like topic mixture percentages (70% Sports + 30% Business = 100%).

"Allocation"

The process of assigning/allocating words to topics.

Simplified Intuition — the Generative Story

LDA assumes documents were "generated" through this imagined process (in reality documents are already written — this is only the mental model behind the math):

  1. For each document, first randomly decide a topic mixture (e.g. "this document will be 70% Sports, 30% Business").
  2. Then, to generate each word in the document, first pick a topic according to that mixture (roll a die weighted 70/30 toward Sports), then pick a word from that topic's word distribution (from the Sports topic, "match" is likely to be picked).

LDA then works backward from real documents to infer the most probable topic distributions and word distributions that would have generated the text we actually observe.

Analogy: each document is a smoothie made by blending different fruit purees (topics) in different proportions. LDA is a food scientist who, given only the finished smoothie (the document), tries to figure out which fruits (topics) were blended and in what proportions — purely by analyzing the flavor profile (word patterns), without ever seeing the blending process directly.
NLP

Practical Steps (with sklearn / Gensim)

  1. Preprocess text — lowercase, remove stopwords, remove punctuation, lemmatize. All the preprocessing steps from earlier chapters apply here too; clean text matters a lot for good topics.
  2. Convert to a numeric representation — typically Bag of Words or TF-IDF is used as input to LDA. LDA itself works on raw word counts/frequencies, not embeddings like Word2Vec.
  3. Choose the number of topics (k) — a key hyperparameter you must decide in advance (e.g. k=5). Too few topics merges distinct themes together; too many splits a single theme into overly narrow fragments.
  4. Train LDA — the algorithm iteratively refines its guess of topic-word distributions and document-topic distributions until it converges on a stable solution.
  5. Interpret topics manually — look at the top-weighted words in each discovered topic and assign a human-readable label (e.g. top words goal/match/team/player → label it "Sports").
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.decomposition import LatentDirichletAllocation

cv = CountVectorizer(stop_words='english', max_df=0.9, min_df=2)
X = cv.fit_transform(documents)   # documents = list of preprocessed texts

lda = LatentDirichletAllocation(n_components=5, random_state=42)  # 5 topics
lda.fit(X)

# print top words for each discovered topic
words = cv.get_feature_names_out()
for idx, topic in enumerate(lda.components_):
    top_words = [words[i] for i in topic.argsort()[-10:]]
    print(f"Topic {idx}: {top_words}")

lda.components_ holds one row per topic, giving each vocabulary word's weight under that topic; sorting a row and taking the highest-weighted indices gives that topic's most representative words — the same words a human then reads to decide what to call the topic.

NLP

Other Topic Modeling Techniques (Good to Know)

LSA (Latent Semantic Analysis)

An older technique using matrix factorization (SVD) on the TF-IDF matrix — conceptually similar to GloVe's approach for word embeddings, but applied at the document-topic level instead. Generally considered less interpretable and lower quality than LDA.

NMF (Non-negative Matrix Factorization)

Another matrix-factorization-based alternative to LDA — often produces more interpretable topics on smaller datasets, and doesn't rely on the probabilistic Dirichlet assumptions LDA uses.

BERTopic

A modern approach that uses BERT-based embeddings (contextual, semantic) combined with clustering, instead of relying purely on word co-occurrence counts like LDA. Generally produces higher-quality, more coherent topics on modern text (social media, complex documents), because it leverages the deep semantic understanding of transformer embeddings rather than just statistical word-frequency patterns.

Test Your Understanding

Hard

5 questions on Topic Modeling. Pick an answer for each, then check.

1. Why is topic modeling classified as unsupervised, unlike POS tagging or NER?

Explanation: POS tagging and NER are supervised — trained on examples humans already tagged. Topic modeling has no labels at all; it discovers structure purely from statistical patterns of word co-occurrence across documents, which a human only interprets afterward.

2. Under the topic modeling assumption, which statement best describes a "topic"?

Explanation: A topic is a distribution over words — e.g. under the "Sports" topic, "goal" might have weight 0.08 while "election" has weight 0.0001. It is not one fixed keyword.

3. In LDA's generative story, what happens right after a document's topic mixture (e.g. 70% Sports / 30% Business) is decided?

Explanation: In LDA's imagined generative process, once a document's topic mixture is decided, each word is generated by first picking a topic according to that mixture (like a weighted dice roll), then picking a word from that topic's word distribution.

4. What does LDA take as input, and what does it explicitly NOT use?

Explanation: LDA works on Bag of Words / TF-IDF style raw word counts or frequencies. It does not take embeddings like Word2Vec as input — that's a different representation used elsewhere in the NLP pipeline.

5. In the sklearn snippet lda.fit(X) followed by lda.components_, what does sorting a row of lda.components_ and taking the highest-weighted indices give you?

Explanation: Each row of lda.components_ gives a topic's weight over every vocabulary word. Sorting and taking the top indices yields that topic's most representative words — exactly what a human reads to decide the topic's label, e.g. goal/match/team/player → "Sports".
